Chapter One: Four Children in the Agora
When I was living in fifth-century Athens, a remarkable thing happened. I was doing what I always do — standing in the Agora, watching the citizens go about their important business. The fishmongers were arguing over prices. A general was telling a large crowd of admirers about a battle he had won, though I noticed that the details of the victory grew more impressive each time I heard the telling. Politicians were making promises they understood no better than the people who believed them. Everything was as it always is, and I was thinking, as I often think, that the city hums with the noise of people who know exactly what they're doing.

It was in the middle of all this that four children appeared.

They did not appear out of thin air — I want to be clear about that, because people who heard the story later added that detail, as people always add details that seem as though they ought to be true. They walked through the Propylaia gate like any visitor to the city. What was unusual was not how they arrived but how they looked: they looked like people who had just set something very heavy down and were only beginning to notice how tired their arms were.

The oldest girl found me first. She was perhaps thirteen, dark-eyed, the kind of child who watches before she speaks. "You are Socrates," she said. It was not a question.

I told her that this was what people called me, yes, though I had never been entirely certain it was the most accurate description.

She stared at me. "We are not from Athens," she said. "We are not from the fifth century."

Well. I have encountered many unusual arguments in the Agora. I looked at the four of them for a long moment.

Then I said: "Tell me what kind of trouble."

They told me. I will not reproduce everything they said because some of it I did not fully understand — they spoke of things I have no word for, mechanisms and arrangements of their time that have no equivalent in mine. But the shape of the difficulty was entirely familiar to me. Their time, it seemed, was full of people who were absolutely certain they were right. Different people, certain of different things, each group convinced that its certainty was the correct one and that all the other certainties were not merely wrong but threatening. And because everyone was certain, no one was listening. And because no one was listening, the strife was growing.

I had to sit down on the nearest step. Not from shock — from recognition.

"Dear children," I said, "you have traveled a very long distance to find a problem that is entirely at home in fifth-century Athens."

Their faces fell.

The decisive one — Pallas, they called her — said: "If you are not willing to help us, what can we do?"

I told her what I always tell people when they accuse me of unhelpfulness: I explained about the oracle, and about my wisdom, and about how my wisdom consists only in this — that I am aware of how little I actually know, whereas most people are not even aware of that. I thought this would satisfy them. It never satisfies anyone, but I always think it will.
