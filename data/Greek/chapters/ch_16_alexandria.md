Chapter 16: All the Books in the World

We had moved forward in time. I want to be clear about this, because I myself only understood it slowly, and even now, sitting in this cell, I am not sure I have understood it fully.

Klēta explained it to me on the deck as the coastline of Egypt rose ahead of us. She said the boat could sail not only against the wind but, in some smaller way, against the years. She did not say how. I have learned to stop asking children how their machines work; they answer me, and then I am no wiser, only more politely confused. What I understood was this: the city we were approaching had not yet been built in my own time. Alexander, the young Macedonian whom I had heard described once or twice as a promising student of Aristotle, had grown up, conquered the known world, and founded this city on the Egyptian coast before dying very young. The children said this had happened a generation or so after my own death. They said it as gently as they could.

So I was looking at a city that did not exist.

It was magnificent. I will say that plainly, because a man who refuses to admit when something is magnificent is not a philosopher but a grump. Alexandria had been designed from nothing, all at once, by people who knew exactly what they wanted: wide straight streets, a harbor that could shelter a thousand ships, a lighthouse so tall that I at first mistook it for a mountain. Behind the city stretched the Nile delta, that strange river which Herodotus had described in such loving detail, the river that flows from south to north and floods on a schedule and feeds half the world with grain. Far to the south, almost lost in haze, I could see the pyramids. They had been ancient when Herodotus saw them. They were ancient still.

But it was not the lighthouse or the pyramids that made Phoebe go quiet. It was the Library.

We were taken there by a librarian — a thin man with ink on his fingers who spoke six languages and apologized for not speaking more. He led us through halls of shelves, and the shelves held scrolls, and the scrolls were numbered, and the numbers were written in a catalog, and the catalog was itself a kind of book. The librarian explained, with a pride he tried to keep modest, that the Library aimed to collect every book ever written, in every language, from every people. Ships entering the harbor were searched not for contraband but for scrolls; any book found was copied, and sometimes the copy was returned and the original kept. He said this as though it were a small embarrassment.

Phoebe stood in the middle of one of the long halls and did not move for a long time.

"Socrates," she said finally, "this is the most serious thing I have ever seen."

I asked her what she meant.

"They are trying," she said. "They are actually trying. To know everything. To put it all in one place so that anyone who has a question can find the answer."

I sat down on a low bench. I do this when something strikes me, and something had struck me. Because she was right. This was not the bluster of a general or the certainty of a priest. This was a sober, patient, enormous attempt at truth. I admired it. I want to say that clearly before I say anything else.

Dolios had wandered off and came back carrying a scroll with both arms. "Look," he said. "Daedalus. There is a whole scroll about Daedalus. About the labyrinth. About the wings." His face was lit up the way it had been at Knossos, but more so, because here was someone who had written it down, who had collected the stories he had only seen in fragments.

Klēta found scrolls about ships, about pulleys, about the mathematics of curves. Pallas found Thucydides on the war that had ruined my city. And Phoebe found Herodotus, the long second book, the one entirely about Egypt — about the Nile, about the priests, about the customs of a people Herodotus had loved enough to write about for hundreds of pages.

"We could stay here," Phoebe said. "We could read until we found it. The thing we came looking for. It must be here. If it is anywhere, it is here."

I did not argue. I asked the librarian a question instead.

"Friend," I said, "if I came to you with a question — a real question, the kind a person asks when they are trying to live well — could you tell me which scroll contains the answer?"

The librarian thought about this. He was an honest man; I could tell by how long he took.

"I could tell you," he said slowly, "which scrolls discuss the question. I could bring you Plato, and Aristotle, and the Stoics, and the Egyptian priests, and the Hebrew prophets, and the teachers from India whose books we have only just begun to translate. I could bring you a hundred scrolls."

"And which of them would be right?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he smiled, a little sadly.

"That," he said, "is not in the catalog."

Phoebe was still holding Herodotus. She did not put it down. But she looked at the scroll differently now, the way one looks at a beloved friend who has just admitted they cannot help with the thing one most needs help with.

We walked out of the Library into the long Egyptian evening. The lighthouse was beginning to burn. Somewhere to the south, the pyramids stood in their enormous patience, having outlasted every civilization that had ever tried to explain them.

"There is so much here," Phoebe said. "And it isn't enough."

I did not answer. I was thinking about how a city built from nothing, all at once, by a young king who believed he was the son of a god, looked very much like the answer to every question — until you asked it one.
