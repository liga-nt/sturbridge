Chapter 0: The Storm

I did not see what I am about to describe. I want to say that plainly, before I say anything else. A man writing about a storm he did not stand inside is already at a disadvantage, and the children told me this story in pieces over many days, sometimes contradicting one another about which wave came first or who was at the tiller when the sky changed. So what follows is my reconstruction. Take it as such.

They had taken the boat without permission. This much is certain, because all four of them admitted it independently, and children who agree on the wrongness of a thing they did together are usually telling the truth. Their world — the one they had not yet described to me in detail — was, by their accounts, loud and frightened and full of people shouting past each other. Pallas said she simply wanted an hour of quiet. Dolios said he wanted to see how fast the boat would go. Klēta said she wanted to test some adjustment she had made to the rigging. Phoebe said she went because the others were going and she did not trust them to come back.

The sea, that morning, was the Aegean — though not the Aegean I know. Same water, they assured me, same coastline in its broad shape, but emptier of fishing boats and louder, somehow, with engines I do not understand. They sailed out past the headlands into open water. The sun was high. The wind was steady. For perhaps an hour they were happy.

Then the sky went black.

I asked them, more than once, to describe how it happened, because I have lived by the sea all my life and I know how storms gather. They build. You see them coming for an hour, sometimes a day. This storm, they said, did not build. It arrived. One moment the horizon was clean, and the next a wall of cloud stood over them, black as wet stone, and the wind that had been carrying them began to push them sideways and then up and then down in a way that wind is not supposed to do.

Pallas took the tiller. Klēta went to the ropes. Dolios, who in calmer moments would have made a joke, made no joke. Phoebe, the watchful one, told me later that she remembered thinking the wind had a voice — not a sound, she said, but a voice, the way a person has a voice — and that the voice was not angry with them exactly, but was paying attention to them in a way she did not want to be paid attention to.

This is the point at which I must stop and tell you what I think happened, though I will not insist on it. We Athenians have a name for that voice. Poseidon, who shakes the earth and lifts the sea, does not always announce himself. Sometimes he is simply the weather. The children did not know, in that moment, that there is a god whose business is the sea. They learned the name later, from me, and were quiet for some time after they learned it.

The waves became walls. The boat was thrown. Klēta said at one point she looked up and saw water above her — not a wave breaking, but water suspended over the boat as though the sea had decided, briefly, to be the sky. Dolios was washed off the deck and caught by a rope around his ankle and hauled himself back aboard with both hands and, he claims, his teeth. Pallas stayed at the tiller. Phoebe, who is small and was tied to the mast, watched.

And then it stopped.

Not gradually. Stopped. The wind fell off all at once, and the sea, which had been mountainous, lay down flat as a beaten bronze plate. The black cloud was gone. The sun was where it had been before. And the coastline they could see in the distance was not the coastline they had set out from.

It was mine. They did not know that yet, of course. They sailed toward it because there was nowhere else to sail, and because the boat — and here is what I want to pause on — the boat was undamaged. Sail intact. Rigging intact. That extraordinary rope-pattern, of which I will speak in a moment, intact. Whatever had happened to them, the vessel had been spared.

Now. About the rigging. I have already told you that when Klēta showed me the boat at Piraeus I had to sit down, and I will not repeat that scene. But I want to say here, in this chapter, what struck me so forcefully. In my time, when a captain needs a wind he does not have, he waits. Sometimes he waits for days. Sometimes he waits so long that terrible things are done in the waiting — Agamemnon waited at Aulis and, unable to sail into a contrary wind, sacrificed his own daughter Iphigenia to obtain a favorable one. A whole tragedy, a whole house cursed, because no Greek captain in that age could do what these four children's small boat could do as a matter of course: move against the wind.

I think about this often. I think how many of our oldest stories are stories of waiting for a wind. And I think how, if Agamemnon had owned the rope-pattern of a ten-year-old girl from a future I cannot picture, his daughter would have lived.

But this is my musing, not the children's story. They washed up on a beach east of Piraeus. They climbed out of the boat. They did not yet know what year it was, or what country, or what god had had them in his hand. They only knew that they were alive, and that the world they had left was somehow no longer the world they were standing in.

They walked up toward the city. And the next morning, as I have told you, they found me on the steps of the Agora.
