Chapter 11: The Cycle That Invented Justice

We came up from Nauplia by the road that runs north into the hills, and the children were quiet, which I had learned to recognize as the silence that comes before a place announces itself.

Mycenae announced itself.

It sat on its hill above the Argive plain like a lion that has grown old but still remembers being a lion. The walls were enormous — great unshaped stones piled by hands that no longer existed. In my own day we called them cyclopean, because no one believed mortal men could have lifted them, and so we said the Cyclopes must have done it. This is the way of human beings. When we cannot explain a thing, we attribute it to giants.

We climbed to the gate. Two lions, carved in stone, flanked a column above the lintel — heraldic, watchful, their heads long since lost to weather and time. Pallas stopped beneath them and put her hand against the stone.

"Who lived here?" she said.

I told her. Perseus, the stories say, founded this city. He was the boy sent to fetch the head of the Gorgon Medusa — she whose gaze turned men to stone. He flew on winged sandals (here Dolios pricked up his ears, and I pretended not to notice), looked only at her reflection in his polished shield, and struck. From her blood sprang the winged horse Pegasus. He carried her head home and used it, when he needed to, as a weapon. Then he founded Mycenae.

"He founded a city," Klēta said slowly, "with a severed head."

I told her this was a thing worth noticing.

We walked through the gate. Inside, the citadel was a quiet ruin — shaft graves where kings had been buried with masks of beaten gold, foundations of palaces no one had walked in for seven hundred years. I sat down on a low wall and told them the rest of the story, because the rest of the story is what Mycenae is for.

Agamemnon was king here, I said. He was the one whose fleet was becalmed at Aulis — the fleet that could not tack — and who sacrificed his own daughter Iphigeneia to obtain favorable winds. He sailed for Troy. He won the war. He came home in triumph, and his wife Clytemnestra, who had not forgiven the murder of her daughter, met him with smiles and a bath and an axe. She killed him in his own house. Then their son Orestes, when he was grown, killed her in turn, to avenge his father. Then the Furies — the old goddesses of blood-vengeance — pursued Orestes for killing his mother.

Phoebe had her hand over her mouth.

"It doesn't stop," she said.

"It does not stop," I said. "That is the point of the story."

I told them about Aeschylus, who had taken these old tales and shaped them into three plays — the Oresteia — performed in Athens when I was a young man. In the final play the cycle is broken. Athena descends. She establishes a court — the Areopagus, that bare limestone outcrop where I now go to pray — and gives the case of Orestes to twelve human judges. They vote. The vote is tied. Athena casts the deciding vote for acquittal. The Furies are persuaded — slowly, reluctantly — to become the Eumenides, the Kindly Ones, and to accept honor in the city instead of feeding on revenge.

"That," said Pallas, "is what we need."

I asked her what she meant.

"Courts," she said. "Real ones. Where you bring the violence and the violence stops. Where the cycle ends because there is a process instead of an axe."

She was lit up. I had not seen her so awake since Thermopylae. The thing that excited her about courage and sacrifice now excited her about process and law: the idea that human beings could build a thing larger than themselves and stand inside it and be saved from each other.

We walked out of the citadel and down the slope to the Treasury of Atreus — a great beehive tomb cut into the hillside, its doorway crowned by a single lintel stone weighing more than any stone has a right to weigh. Inside, the dome rose above us in concentric rings, each course slightly smaller than the last, until the rings closed at the top into a single point. Klēta stood in the center looking up and made a small sound of pure joy. Whoever built this, I thought, had understood something true about how stones want to lean against each other.

We walked from there along the road to the Heraion — the sanctuary of Hera that lay just to the south, older than almost any sanctuary in Greece. Inside stood the great cult statue, gold and ivory, made by Polycleitus. Hera here was not the jealous wife of the comic poets. She was the goddess who held oaths together, who made marriages binding, who guarded the agreements without which no household and no city could stand. Clytemnestra, when she struck Agamemnon down, had broken everything Hera presided over. The whole tragic cycle began with a broken oath.

We sat on the temple steps. The afternoon light was long and gold across the plain.

"Pallas," I said, "tell me about Athena's vote."

She looked at me.

"In the play," I said. "The human judges tied. Six and six. The cycle would not have ended on the human vote alone. It ended because a god broke the tie. The court worked because divine wisdom was inside it."

She was quiet for a long time.

"Then where does the wisdom come from," she said at last, "in a court without gods?"

I did not answer her. I had no answer to give. We sat together on the steps of Hera's temple and watched the shadows move across the plain where Agamemnon's chariots had once mustered, and I thought how strange it was that the oldest human problem was still the newest one.
