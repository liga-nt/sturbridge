Chapter R1: What the Jury Decided

I came down the steps of the court with the verdict still ringing in my ears, and there they were. Four children, salt-stained and breathless, pushing through the crowd as though the crowd were a hedge they had decided to walk through.

I confess I was glad to see them. A man who has just been sentenced to death is permitted, I think, a small private pleasure at the return of friends.

"You're alive," Pallas said.

"For the moment," I said. "The Athenians have decided that this should not continue indefinitely."

"They convicted you?" Klēta's face went tight.

I told her the count. Two hundred and eighty against me, two hundred and twenty for me. A closer thing than my accusers had hoped. Then the jury had asked what penalty I proposed for myself, and I had suggested that the city should feed me at public expense for the rest of my life, as it fed its Olympic victors. This, I explained to the children, had not been received in the spirit in which it was offered. The second vote, on the sentence, had gone considerably worse for me than the first.

"Free meals," Dolios said. He started to grin and then stopped, uncertain whether grinning was permitted.

"It seemed to me," I said, "that if I had spent my life doing the city a service, the city might acknowledge it. The jury disagreed."

A young man stood a little apart from us, very pale, watching me without speaking. That was Plato. He had been in the court the whole morning. He would, I knew, write some of this down later, and I hoped he would write down the meals part, because I had meant it, and I did not want anyone to imagine I had been joking.

Phoebe had not spoken yet. She was looking at me with the particular expression of a person who has been carrying news a long way and is afraid the news will not survive the delivery.

"Socrates," she said. "We went to Delphi."

I sat down on the lowest step. "Tell me."

She told me. She told me about the climb up Parnassus, and the priestess on her tripod over the cleft in the rock, and a golden light she had seen — or felt, she corrected herself, or something between seeing and feeling. She said it with great care. She said it the way one speaks of something one is afraid of breaking.

"And what did the light tell you?" I asked.

She opened her mouth and then closed it.

"That the gods are real," she said finally. "That there is knowledge. That someone, somewhere, knows."

"The light said all that?"

"The light meant all that."

"Ah," I said. "Meant. That is a different word."

She watched me.

"Phoebe," I said, "the oracle once spoke about me. It said no one in Athens was wiser than Socrates. I have spent thirty years trying to understand what those words meant. The oracle spoke; I am still interpreting. Do you see the difficulty? The god speaks, and then the human being must decide what was said. And to decide what was said, the human being requires —"

"Wisdom," she said quietly.

"Which is the thing we were looking for in the first place."

She sat down on the step beside me. The Agora went on shouting around us, men arguing about my verdict, men who an hour ago had not known my name.

"So the light was nothing?" she said.

"I did not say that. The light was something. You saw it. I believe you saw it. What it was — that is another question entirely, and not one the light itself can answer for you."

Pallas had been listening with her arms folded. "Socrates," she said. "The jury. Why did they convict you? Did they really think you were guilty?"

This was a good question, and I told her so. I had been thinking about it during the speeches.

"I do not believe most of them thought I was guilty in any precise sense," I said. "I believe my questions made them uncomfortable. I have spent my life going up to people the city admires — generals, poets, politicians. Pericles himself, in the great years of the empire, when Athens ruled the sea and the tribute came in from a hundred cities — Pericles could build the Parthenon, but when I asked him once what made a leader genuinely good, he could not say. He grew irritated. Most people grow irritated. They are certain they know, and they do not, and being shown this is unpleasant."

"So they killed you for that," Klēta said.

"They have not killed me yet. But yes. Certainty, not truth, decided the verdict. The jurors were certain I was a nuisance. On that point, in fairness, they were correct."

Phoebe was still looking at the ground. "I wanted to bring you something," she said. "I wanted to bring you an answer."

"You brought me a question," I said. "That is better. Answers are common in Athens. Good questions are rare."

I stood up. My knees were not what they had been.

"Children," I said. "I have, by the court's calculation, only a handful of days remaining. The ship that carries condemned men to Delos has not yet returned, and while it is away no execution may be carried out — but it will return. You should travel quickly. Whatever you are looking for, you had better look for it before the hemlock cup is brought."

They stared at me.

"Where should we go next?" Pallas asked.

I thought about it. "Sparta," I said. "You have asked the gods. Now ask the men who do not trust the gods at all, and trust only their own courage. See what they have to teach you."
