Chapter 15: The Width of the World

We left Crete with a steady wind from the north, and for the first time since our journey began I had the sensation of being very small.

I am an Athenian. I have spent seventy years walking between the same hills, drinking from the same springs, arguing with the same men in the same Agora. The Aegean, for me, has always been a kind of large room — Athens at one end, Ionia at the other, the islands placed between like furniture. A man can know such a room. He can name what is in it.

But south of Crete the sea opens. It does not feel like a room any longer. It feels like a floor that has lost its walls.

Klēta tried to show me on a chart she had drawn. To the southeast, she said, lay Rhodes — and indeed I could see it dimly, a long mountainous shape, the kind of island that grows rich because every ship between Egypt and the Aegean must take notice of it. I knew Rhodes. I had met Rhodians. They struck me as people who understood money the way Spartans understood war.

"And to the west," Klēta said, sweeping her small hand across her chart, "is the Ionian Sea, and beyond that Sicily, and beyond that the Adriatic, which leads up between Italy and Illyria into waters no Athenian I know has ever sailed."

I sat down on the deck. This was becoming a habit on this voyage.

Sicily I had heard of all my life, of course. It is where ambitious Athenians went to die. I remembered the great expedition — two hundred ships, thousands of hoplites, Alcibiades smiling and Nicias praying — sent west to conquer Syracuse. They did not conquer Syracuse. Syracuse conquered them. The harbor swallowed our fleet, and the quarries swallowed our soldiers, and the city has never been the same since. When Athenians speak of Sicily now, they speak quietly, the way one speaks of a relative who drowned.

And yet, Klēta told me, Sicily is full of Greeks. Greek cities, Greek temples, Greek poets. Greek farmers complaining about Greek weather in Greek. There is a whole second Hellas out there, west of everything I know, and beyond it a third — colonies in Italy, colonies in Gaul, colonies as far as Iberia. And to the north of Sicily, the Adriatic, narrow and strange, leading to peoples I have only heard rumors of.

"How far does it go?" I asked.

Dolios answered, without turning from the rail. "Farther than you think. Farther than anyone here thinks."

He had been quiet since Knossos. The flush of his confidence had drained out of him there, on the wall in front of the unfinished frieze, and it had not come back. He stood now at the prow and watched the water, and I noticed that he was not looking for anything in particular. He was simply looking. This is, in my experience, what people do when they have begun to suspect that the world is larger than their plans for it.

The sea here is a different sea. The Aegean is busy — every wave seems to know where it is going, and every island is a familiar face. This water is older. It does not hurry. It does not announce itself. Beneath us, Klēta said, the bottom drops away to depths that no anchor will ever reach.

I thought of Poseidon. In Athens we speak of him casually, as we speak of a neighbor — the god who shakes the earth when he is annoyed, who sent the bull to Minos, who quarreled with Athena over our city and lost. But out here he is not a character. He is what we are floating on. He is the dark pressure beneath the planks. When a wave lifts us, he lifts us. When the boat rolls, he rolls it. I had been on his back this whole voyage, I realized, and only now was I beginning to feel his weight.

Pallas came and sat beside me. She had been quieter too, since the Apology. She looked toward Rhodes and then toward the haze where Sicily would be, if one's eyes were good enough.

"It is so much," she said.

"Yes," I said.

"All of it Greek. Or some of it. The cities, I mean."

"Some of it."

"And someone is going to want to gather it together," she said. "Aren't they? Sooner or later. Someone is going to look at all of this and want to make it one thing."

I thought about this. I thought about Pericles, who had wanted to make the Aegean one thing, and what that had cost. I thought about the Sicilian expedition. I thought about how every map a man draws becomes, sooner or later, a map he wants to color in.

"Probably," I said. "Men with that ambition seem to come along regularly. Usually they are very young, and very gifted, and very sure."

Dolios, at the prow, did not turn around. But I saw his shoulders move — a small motion, the kind a person makes when something has been said about him that he does not yet wish to acknowledge.

The wind held. The boat ran south. Somewhere ahead of us, Klēta said, was a city built by such a young man. We would be there in two days.

I sat on the deck a while longer, watching the width of the world go by.
