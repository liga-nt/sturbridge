Chapter 13: The Place Where History Crosses

We sailed northeast along the coast of Asia Minor for two days, and on the third morning the land closed in on either side and the sea became a river, or what looked like one. This was the Hellespont — the strait that separates Europe from Asia, a place so narrow that a strong swimmer could cross it, and so important that armies have spent themselves trying.

I had never been there before. I had only heard about it from men who had. But when our boat slipped between the two shores, I understood at once why everyone who writes about this strait writes about it as though it were a living thing. The water moved in two directions at once. On the surface it ran south, out toward the Aegean. Underneath, Pallas told me, looking over the side with her hand trailing in the cold, it ran north, up toward the Black Sea. Two currents, one above the other, going opposite ways. The boundary between worlds.

"Xerxes bridged this," Phoebe said quietly. She was holding her spyglass but had not raised it.

"With boats," I said. "Hundreds of them, lashed together. He marched his whole army across — Persians, Medes, Bactrians, Indians — to invade Greece. The first storm broke the bridge apart. They say he had the water whipped for its disobedience and then built another."

"Whipped the water," Klēta repeated, as though making sure she had heard correctly.

"Three hundred lashes, by some accounts. He also threw chains into it." I shrugged. "A king who believes the sea will be embarrassed by chains is a king who has stopped listening to anyone honest."

Dolios was standing at the bow, looking ahead with that particular forward lean he gets when something has caught his ambition. "And someone will cross it the other way someday," he said. "Won't they? Someone will go from here into Persia."

I admit I looked at him sharply. He spoke as though he already knew. Perhaps in his time he did. He told me later, when I asked, about a Macedonian king named Alexander who would cross this strait a few generations after my death, going east instead of west, weeping at the tomb of Achilles, conquering as far as the rivers of India before dying young. Macedon, in my day, was a rough kingdom on the northern edge of the Greek world, not yet the thing it would become. I filed this away to think about later. The future, when children describe it, often sounds like a bad dream you cannot quite remember.

We anchored that afternoon below the low hill of Troy.

It is not what people expect. The great city of the Iliad is gone — burned three thousand years ago, if Homer is to be believed at all — and what stands there now is a small inhabited town built on the rubble of the older one, with shrines for pilgrims and a few priests who will, for a coin, show you the spot where Achilles dragged Hector behind his chariot. From the height of the citadel you can see the whole plain of the Troad, with the Scamander winding down to the sea, and to the northeast the dark wide line of the Black Sea, opening out beyond the strait toward lands the Greeks have only begun to know.

Dolios stood on the wall and would not come down for a long time. I let him stand. There are some places where children need to be left alone to feel what they are feeling.

That evening we ate at an inn kept by a Phrygian, an old man with one bad eye who served us bread and cheese and a thin wine. When he learned that the children had asked about Achilles, he laughed.

"Everyone asks about Achilles," he said. "Nobody asks about Midas. And Midas was Phrygian. My king, you understand."

So he told them the story, which I had heard before but never in the mouth of a man who claimed Midas as kin. How Midas had done a kindness for the god Dionysus and been offered any wish. How he had asked that everything he touched turn to gold. How for one morning he was the happiest man alive — turning leaves to gold, turning his cup to gold, turning the wooden arm of his chair to gold. How at lunch the bread became gold in his mouth and the wine became gold on his tongue. How his daughter ran to embrace him and became a gold statue in his arms.

"He got exactly what he wanted," the innkeeper said. "That was the punishment."

Dolios was very quiet.

I asked the children, as we walked back to the boat under a sky thick with stars, what Achilles had wanted. Pallas answered first — glory, a name that would last forever. I said that was true, and that he had received it. We were sitting at this very moment under his name. Three thousand years, and we were still saying it.

"But," I said, and then I stopped, because I did not want to lecture, and because I had only a question and not an answer. "The Iliad begins with his wrath. Do you remember? Sing, goddess, the wrath of Achilles. And because of that wrath, his own companions die. Patroclus dies. He gets his glory. But the road to it is paved with the bodies of people he loved."

"Like Midas," Klēta said.

"Perhaps."

Dolios said nothing. He was looking back toward the plain, where somewhere in the dark the Scamander was running to the sea.

Before we slept, I thought of one more thing, and I said it aloud, mostly to myself. "Agamemnon's fleet sailed from near here, you know. Or tried to. They were stranded at Aulis because the winds were wrong, and Agamemnon killed his daughter Iphigenia to make Artemis send a fair wind." I looked at our small boat, with its strange rigging that could sail at an angle into any wind at all. "If he had owned a boat like yours, his daughter would have lived."

Pallas made a small sound. Klēta put her hand on the rail of the boat as though to apologize to it.

We slept on deck that night. I lay awake for a long time, listening to the two currents of the Hellespont running in opposite directions beneath us, and thinking about how many things a person could want, and how few of them were worth getting.
