Chapter 5: The Men Who Ran

I should say at once that I was not at Marathon. The battle was fought in the year I was born — I came into the world the same year the men of my city ran across that plain, and so I have always carried the date in me as one carries the date of a father's death, though it was not death but a kind of birth. What I describe now I describe through the children's eyes, and through the words of a man they encountered there, whom I will name in a moment.

The boat brought them from Delphi up the long coast and around the cape, and they landed at Marathon on a morning in late summer. They told me later that the plain was smaller than they had expected — a thin strip of flat ground caught between low hills and the sea, fragrant with fennel, which is what marathon means in our tongue. Beyond the fennel, where the ground turned to sand, they could see the dark shapes of the Persian ships drawn up in long lines on the beach. And rising from the plain, like a small hill that did not belong to the landscape, was the soros: the burial mound of the hundred and ninety-two Athenians who had died there.

The children had arrived after the battle. The dead were already in the mound. The Persians were already gone. But there was a man walking the plain with a wax tablet, stopping to question anyone who would speak with him, and this man was Herodotus of Halicarnassus, who later wrote down everything he could find out about the wars between the Greeks and the Persians. He was not yet famous when the children met him. He was simply a man who wanted to know what had happened, and who suspected that if he did not write it down, no one would.

He was happy to talk. He told the children that Darius, the Great King of Persia, had sent the expedition to punish Athens for helping the Ionian cities revolt against him. He told them that the Athenians had sent a runner to Sparta begging for help, and that the Spartans had answered that they could not march until their religious festival was complete — the moon, they said, was not yet full. He told them that the Athenians had therefore stood alone, with only the men of Plataea beside them, and that they were outnumbered by a great deal — though he was careful, he said, about the numbers, because numbers in war are the first thing that grow.

Then he told them about the run.

The Athenian general Miltiades, he said, had ordered the hoplites to charge the Persian line at a run — not a walk, not a measured advance, but a run, in armor, across nearly a mile of open ground. They did this, Herodotus said, because the Persian archers were deadly at distance, and the only way to survive the arrows was to be inside them before they could be loosed twice. So the Athenians ran. In bronze, in the heat of late summer, with shields that weighed what a small child weighs, they ran toward men who outnumbered them, and they broke the Persian line, and they drove the Persians back into the sea.

Pallas listened to this without moving. She had stood very still at Delphi too, but that had been the stillness of someone uncertain what to do. This was a different stillness. I have seen it on the faces of young soldiers when they first understand what a soldier is.

"They didn't wait," she said.

"They did not," said Herodotus.

"They didn't argue about whether to run. They just ran."

"There was a vote among the generals," Herodotus said, "and it was close. But once it was decided, yes — they ran."

Pallas walked away from him then, out across the plain, toward the soros. The other three followed at a distance. She climbed partway up the mound and stood there looking down at the sea. Phoebe told me later that Pallas's face had changed — that something in it had set, the way clay sets when it goes into the kiln. She had been a girl who acted before thinking. Now she looked like someone who had found, at last, the thing she had been waiting to act for.

"This is it," she said, when the others reached her. "This is what we came for. Not the oracle. This. People who stopped arguing and ran."

Klēta looked at the mound. "A hundred and ninety-two men are under our feet."

"Yes," said Pallas. "And because of them, we are standing here."

Dolios, who could usually be relied on for a quick remark, said nothing. He was looking at the Persian ships on the beach and thinking, I suspect, about how a small thing done bravely could become a large thing in the telling.

Phoebe raised her spyglass. She told me later that she pointed it at the soros and saw only a mound of earth, and that she pointed it at Pallas and saw a girl with shining eyes. She did not yet know what to do with what the spyglass was showing her. She lowered it without speaking.

That evening, by their boat, Pallas said the thing she had been turning over all afternoon. "Courage," she said. "That's the answer. That's what our people have lost. Not piety, not oracles. Courage. The willingness to run toward the thing, even when you might die."

The others did not argue with her. They were tired, and the soros at their backs was very persuasive.

What none of them asked, and what Herodotus, careful man, did not say — though I think he knew — was the question that always trails behind courage like a shadow trails a runner. Courage toward what? The men under that mound had run toward something. But what they had saved, and what Athens would build with what they had saved, were not yet the same question. They would become the same question, in time. They are the same question now. But Pallas, on that evening, was not ready to ask it. She had found her hope. She held it the way a child holds a coal that has not yet begun to burn.
