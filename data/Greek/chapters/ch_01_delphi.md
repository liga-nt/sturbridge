Chapter 1: What the Oracle Said

I did not go to Delphi with the children. A man bound for trial does not sail off to Mount Parnassus, however curious he may be about what four travelers from the future will say to a priestess who breathes vapors from the rocks. So what follows I have from them — pieced together later, on a different afternoon, when they returned to me with their faces full of questions they had not yet learned how to ask. I tell it as they told it.

They sailed west and north along the gulf, the children said, until they came to Kirrha and climbed up. Anyone who has been to Delphi knows the feeling of that climb. The mountain rises so steeply that the sky seems to lean away from you. The terraces are cut into the rock as though some patient god had taken a chisel to the world and made room for a temple. The air is thin and cold even in summer. Eagles ride the wind above the cliffs of the Phaedriades, and far below, the olive groves of the plain run all the way to the sea.

The children walked up the Sacred Way. Dolios, of course, was reading every dedication aloud as though he had built them himself. Here was the Athenian treasury, raised after Marathon, when our grandfathers turned back the Persian king Darius and his commanders at the plain north of Athens. Here was a great bronze dedicated after Salamis, when the wooden walls the oracle had promised turned out to be ships, and Themistocles understood the riddle in time to save Greece from Xerxes. Here were tripods and shields and a thousand small bronze gods, each one a quiet boast: we won, and we want the god to remember.

"Every battle here was won," Dolios said.

Phoebe, the watchful one, said: "Every battle here that was won."

This is the kind of remark one does not forget. The losers do not leave dedications. The losers walked home, if they walked home at all, and what they had to say about the oracle was not preserved in bronze.

At the temple itself the four of them stopped. Above the entrance, cut into the stone in letters older than my grandfather, were two words. Phoebe read them aloud.

Γνῶθι σεαυτόν.

Know thyself.

She read them again, more slowly. She told me later that she had expected the temple of the god who knows everything to say something else. Knowest thou the god, perhaps. Or, Here are the answers. Instead, the inscription handed the question back to whoever stood at the door.

Inside, at the heart of the sanctuary, sat the omphalos — a stone shaped like a great egg, carved with a net of woolen bands. The Greeks call it the navel of the world. The story is that Zeus released two eagles from the ends of the earth, and where they met, that was the center, and the center was here. The children stared at it. Klēta, who likes to know how things are made, walked all the way around it twice. Pallas wanted to know whether the priests believed it was truly the center, or whether they only said so. Dolios was already at work on a priest.

I should say something about Dolios at Delphi. He had decided that if these were the men who guarded the god's mouth, he would befriend them. He asked what their robes were made of. He admired the bronzes. He told one priest that his beard was the most distinguished beard he had seen in any city. The priest, who had probably heard worse flattery from kings, allowed himself a small smile and let the children stand closer to the inner sanctuary than visitors usually stand. This is how Dolios moves through the world. I do not entirely approve. But I notice that doors open for him.

Phoebe, meanwhile, had drawn out the spyglass.

She had found it on the boat before they left Piraeus, tucked under a bench, as though it had been waiting for her. Bronze, she said, with a sun chased into the side in fine lines. She did not know what it was for. She had brought it because she could not bring herself not to.

Now, in the forecourt of Apollo's temple, she raised it to her eye and turned it toward the sanctuary.

She told me afterward that she did not know what she expected. A clearer view of the cult statue, perhaps. What she saw instead was light. The whole temple flooded with gold, she said — not the gold of the sun on stone, but a different gold, deeper, as though the building itself were burning quietly from within. She could see the omphalos at the center of it, and around the omphalos the light moved like water around a thrown stone. She lowered the spyglass and the light went away. She raised it and the light returned. She did this several times, to be sure.

When she stopped, she was trembling.

"It is here," she whispered to the others. "The truth is here. The god knows. We only have to ask the right question."

Pallas asked her what she had seen. Phoebe could not say. Klēta asked her to describe the light. Phoebe could not describe it. Dolios asked, with his usual practical mind, whether they could now go in and ask the Pythia where civilization had gone wrong, since they were already paying for the trip.

So they went in.

They told me the priestess sat over a chasm in the floor, breathing the vapors that rose from below, and that her voice, when it came, did not sound like a woman's voice. They told me she spoke in verse. They told me her words were these — and I will give them as the children gave them to me, because oracles are not the kind of thing one paraphrases:

The wind that bears you cannot fill the sail.
The wisdom that you seek you must not steal.
The road runs back. The teacher has no scroll.
Know first the questioner, and last the goal.

They came out into the sunlight and stood for a long time without speaking. Then Phoebe said, with great certainty: "I understand it. I understand part of it, anyway. The god is telling us the truth is real. We only need to learn how to hear it."

Pallas said: "It sounded to me like the god was telling us to go home."

Klēta said: "It sounded to me like four different sentences that have nothing to do with each other."

Dolios said: "It sounded expensive."

This is the trouble with oracles. Croesus of Lydia, before the Persian wars, asked the god whether he should attack Cyrus, and the god replied that if he did, he would destroy a great empire. Croesus, delighted, marched east and destroyed a great empire — his own. The Athenians, when Xerxes came, were told to trust their wooden walls, and half the city thought that meant the old palisade on the Acropolis, and the other half, with Themistocles leading them, thought it meant the fleet. The fleet was right. The palisade burned. The oracle had said both things and neither, and the Athenians who chose correctly were the ones who already knew what to do.

But the children did not know any of this yet. They were standing on the slope of Parnassus with the light still in Phoebe's eyes, and they were certain — Phoebe most of all — that they had found the place where truth lived and that they had only to learn how to read it.

They climbed back down to the harbor as the sun was going behind the mountains. Phoebe held the spyglass against her chest the whole way. She told me later that she did not sleep that night. She lay on the deck of their strange boat and watched the stars and thought about the inscription above the temple door.

Know thyself.

She thought it meant: know yourself well enough to ask the right question. She did not yet think it meant anything else.
