Olympia

I had not planned to go with the children on any part of their journey. As I said before, I had never expected to see them again. But when they returned from Delphi with a god's answer that did not answer them, and announced they were sailing for Olympia, I found I could not let them go alone. I knew I would like to see the games one last time but I was even more eager to watch my new companions witness the games with fresh eyes. The generations of people are like trees, the poet says, some leaves falling to the ground while others are pushed forth by the vigour of the sap. My trial was scheduled in one week's time and I had no intention to miss it.

We sailed from Delphi down the gulf and around into the western country, where the rivers run wide and slow and the land flattens out into a green plain. Olympia sits in that plain, between the Alpheios and a smaller river whose name escapes me, and as we came up from the coast I could already hear it — not the sanctuary itself, but the crowd. Every four years the whole Greek world pours itself into that small valley, and the sound of so many people gathered for one purpose carries a long way.

The sacred truce is the thing about Olympia that always struck me most. For the duration of the games, every Greek city stops fighting every other Greek city. Spartans and Athenians who were stabbing each other last month walk past each other without drawing knives. Whatever else the games are, they are at least proof that the Greeks know how to stop, when they decide to.

Phoebe noticed this within the first hour. "Everyone is here," she said. "And nobody is fighting."

"Not today," I said.

"I think we're in the right place," she said. 

We made our way through the crowd toward the Altis, the sacred enclosure where the temples stand. Dolios disappeared almost at once. He came back twenty minutes later having apparently convinced a priest that he was the cousin of a victor from Syracuse, which got us closer to the running track than we had any right to be. Klēta was meanwhile crouching at the base of a column, running her fingers along the joining of the stones and asking me, in a low serious voice, how the Greeks had lifted them. I did not know. I told her so. She seemed pleased that I admitted it.

Pallas watched the athletes. She did not speak for a long time. When a runner from Kroton crossed the line ahead of the others and the crowd lifted him onto their shoulders, she said, almost to herself, "He won. Everyone can see he won. There is no argument."

"No argument at all," I agreed.

"That is what we need," she said. "A way to say who has done well that nobody can dispute."

Phoebe was nodding. I could see the shape of her thought — that if her people could only agree on what counted as excellence, and reward it publicly, the strife she had described to me at the harbor might quiet. It is a beautiful thought which I have had many times myself.

We went into the temple of Zeus.

I have seen the great statue many times, and never remained unmoved. Pheidias made it of gold and ivory, twelve meters of seated god, holding a small Nike in one hand and a scepter in the other with an eagle perched on top. The thunderer, made tender. I have watched grown men weep in front of it. The children stopped at the threshold and went very quiet. Even Dolios stopped grinning.

I sat down on a bench along the wall. I always sit down in front of the Zeus.

After a while I told them the story of Atalanta. Atalanta was the fastest runner in the world. No man could catch her. She had sworn she would marry only the man who could outrun her in a race, and the men who tried and lost were put to death. Many tried. Many lost. Then came Hippomenes, who was not faster than her, but who carried three golden apples given to him by Aphrodite. As they ran, he dropped the apples one by one. Each time, Atalanta turned aside to pick one up. And so he won the race. Not by being faster. By distracting the faster runner.

"That is cheating," said Pallas.

"Is it?" I said. "He crossed the line first. Everyone could see he crossed the line first. There was no argument."

She was quiet.

"Tell me," I said, "who was actually the better runner?"

"Atalanta," said Klēta at once.

"And who won?"

"Hippomenes," said Dolios.

"So the race measured something," I said. "But perhaps not what we thought it measured."

Phoebe was looking up at the gold face of Zeus. I could see her trying to hold two things at once — the grandeur of a place where everyone agreed who had won, and the small uncomfortable question of whether they had agreed about the right thing.

"Socrates," she said finally, "the fastest man — is he also the best man?"

We were walking out of the temple into the late afternoon light. The crowd was still cheering somewhere over by the stadium. Slaves were carrying water jars along the edge of the Altis, their bodies muscular and gleaming in the afternoon sun. Pallas noticed them and answered much better than I could have done. "Maybe the fastest man," she said, "is not even the fastest man." 

The next morning we sailed for Athens.
