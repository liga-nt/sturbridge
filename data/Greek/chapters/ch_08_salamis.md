Chapter 8: The Cleverest Thing

I cannot tell you exactly how the children's boat moved them through time. They tried to explain it to me once, and I nodded the way a man nods when his host explains the recipe for a dish he has been eating with great pleasure. But I can tell you what they saw at Salamis, because they described it to me afterward in such detail that I felt I had stood beside them.

It was the September of the year the Persians came. Xerxes, the Great King, son of Darius, had crossed the Hellespont with an army so large that the historians later argued not about its size but about how to exaggerate it credibly. He had burned Athens. He had driven the population to the island of Salamis, just across a narrow strait from the Attic coast. And on the heights above that strait he had set up a golden throne, so that he might watch his fleet finish what his army had begun.

The children's boat slid into the strait at the hour when the smoke was thickest. Pallas told me afterward that the water was the color of wine and ash mixed together. Persian triremes lay broken in every direction — some still burning, some half-sunk with their oars still extended like the legs of dead insects. The water smelled, she said, of pitch and salt and smoke and something else she could not name and did not wish to.

"Look up there," said Dolios, pointing.

On the headland, very small and very still, sat a figure on a chair that caught the afternoon light. Around him, attendants. Below him, ruin. Phoebe raised her spyglass and reported that the Great King of Persia was not weeping. He was simply watching, the way a man watches a building burn that he himself has set fire to and cannot now put out.

"How?" said Klēta. She was not asking about Xerxes. She was looking at the Greek triremes — smaller, fewer, but moving through the wreckage with the quiet authority of fish that know their pond. "How did they win? They were outnumbered three to one."

A Greek sailor was hauling rope on a nearby vessel and overheard her. He laughed — the harsh laugh of a man who has not yet stopped being afraid. "Themistocles," he said, as though that were the whole answer.

And in a way it was. Themistocles was an Athenian. Ten years before this battle, when there had been a great strike of silver at Laurion, the Athenians had wanted to divide the windfall among themselves — a coin in every citizen's hand. Themistocles had persuaded them instead to build ships. Two hundred triremes. The citizens grumbled. There was no war. What were two hundred ships for?

They were for this day. They were for the strait of Salamis, where Themistocles, having lured the Persian fleet into water too narrow for its numbers to matter, broke it on the rocks of his own foresight.

Klēta listened to all this with her mouth slightly open. I am told she did not speak for a long time. Then she said, very quietly: "He saw it before anyone else did."

"Yes," said the sailor.

"He built the ships before anyone knew they were needed."

"Yes."

She turned to the others. Her face, Phoebe told me later, had the particular brightness of someone who has just found a coin in the road. "This is it," Klēta said. "This is what saves a city. Not prayers. Not even courage by itself. Someone has to *see ahead.* Someone has to be clever enough to build the thing before it is needed."

Dolios, who liked any argument that put intelligence at the center of the world, agreed enthusiastically. Pallas, who had spent the previous days enamored of courage, looked thoughtful and said nothing for a while; courage, she was beginning to suspect, was not enough on its own. Phoebe watched the smoke.

They sailed slowly down the strait, past the wreckage. A Greek captain, seeing their strange rigging, hailed them and asked where they were from. Dolios gave one of his answers — charming, vague, technically not a lie — and the captain laughed and let them pass.

It was as they were leaving the strait that Klēta, still glowing, asked the sailor one more question. "What happened to Themistocles? After. Did Athens make him king? Did they build him a temple?"

The sailor's face did a strange thing. He looked at the deck. He looked at the sea. He said, "That was a long time after, child. Ask someone else."

"But what happened?"

"They drove him out," said the sailor. "Ostracism. Ten years in exile. He went east in the end. He died in Persian service. In the pay of the son of the man whose fleet he sank here."

The children stood very still. The wind was picking up. Behind them, the strait still burned.

"Why?" said Klēta.

The sailor shrugged the shrug of a man who has stopped expecting cities to be grateful. "Athens does what Athens does," he said.

They sailed on. Klēta kept looking back, even after the smoke had become only a smudge on the horizon and the headland with its small still figure had sunk below the waves of the wine-dark Aegean. I think she was trying, even then, to fit the shape of what she had heard into the shape of what she had decided. The cleverest man in Greece had saved Greece. Greece had destroyed him. He had then served the enemy he had defeated.

She did not yet know what to do with that. Neither, in truth, did I, when she told me. I sat down on my stone and thought about it for a long time.
