Chapter 7: No Walls But These

I was not in Sparta, of course. I have never been in Sparta, and now I never shall be. But the children told me what they saw, and they told me carefully — Phoebe in particular, who watches before she speaks, and who had begun by then to suspect that what she saw was not always what was there. So I will tell it as they told it, and where I add something of my own, I will say so.

They came down through the mountains of Laconia and into the valley of the Eurotas, expecting a city. There was no city. There were five villages spread across a plain, and no walls between them, and no walls around them. Pallas asked a man at a well why the city had no walls, and he looked at her as though she had asked why a lion had no shell, and said: "Our young men are the walls." Then he walked away. The children agreed afterward that this was the longest answer they received in Sparta from anyone over the age of seven.

There was no theater. There was no agora in the sense I would understand the word — no place where men stood about arguing and selling fish and being wrong with great enthusiasm. There were no temples to compare with the Parthenon, no sculpted friezes, no painted porches. There was no coined money. Klēta, who notices what is built and how, told me she felt as though someone had taken a city and removed everything from it that was not necessary, and she could not decide whether what remained was admirable or terrifying.

What there was, instead, was boys. They saw them everywhere — in groups, running, wrestling, carrying bundles, eating in silence at long tables outside. The youngest were seven, taken from their mothers at that age and given to the state. This is called the agoge, and I had heard of it, but hearing of a thing and seeing it are different. Pallas watched a pack of boys her own age running barefoot through gravel. One of them was bleeding from the foot and did not slow down. She told me afterward that she had never seen anything so beautiful, and then, a moment later, that she did not know whether beautiful was the right word, but that no other word came.

Dolios tried to charm a Spartan girl by asking what she did for fun. She said: "I run." He asked what else. She said: "I throw the javelin." He asked what else. She walked away. He told me he had never failed so completely with so few words spoken against him.

They came at last to the sanctuary of Ares Enyalios. I asked Phoebe to describe it precisely, because I had heard of this thing all my life and never seen it. She said there was a statue of the god of war, and the statue was bound in iron chains. The chains were not a metaphor. They were real chains, fastened to the base, wrapped around the god's wrists and ankles. The Spartans had explained to her, in the few words they used for explanations, that Ares could never leave Sparta. He was their prisoner. War belonged to them.

Pallas thought this was magnificent. The god of destruction made a servant of the city — what could be more disciplined than that? What could be more like the answer she was looking for? Here was a place that had taken the most dangerous force in the world and put it in chains and made it serve. A people that did this could not be conquered. And in fact, the children knew — for they had read their histories — that Sparta would defeat Athens in the long war that was even now grinding on. At a place called Aegospotami, only a few years from the day they stood there, the Spartan fleet would catch the Athenian fleet at anchor and destroy it, and the long war would end, and Athens would lose everything she had built since Marathon. Pallas knew all this. She stood before the chained god and felt that she had found, at last, the thing her people needed.

Phoebe stood beside her and raised the spyglass.

She did not tell Pallas what she saw. She told me later, when she could find words for it. Through the glass she saw the statue and the chains, and she saw, behind the statue, a long shadow that stretched out behind the city itself — many shadows, the shadows of people, and the people were not Spartans. They were thin, and they were afraid, and they were working in fields she could not see, and there were a great many of them, and none of them spoke. She lowered the glass and the shadows were gone. She raised it again and they returned.

She asked a Spartan woman who those people were, the ones who did the farming, since the Spartans themselves seemed to do nothing but train.

"Helots," the woman said. And then, because Phoebe was still looking at her: "Do not ask."

That night, when the children sat by their fire outside the city, Pallas spoke of discipline and simplicity and the chained god, and how perhaps this was what their people had lost. Klēta listened and said little. Dolios made jokes that no one laughed at. Phoebe sat with the spyglass in her lap and did not raise it again. She told me later she had not known, until that night, that a city could be a weapon, and that a weapon must always be held by a hand, and that the hand must always be pressed against someone's throat.

They sailed the next morning. Pallas was still certain. The others were not. And I, who was not there, sit now in my cell and write what they told me, and I think about the chains on the god, and I wonder which of us was the prisoner.
