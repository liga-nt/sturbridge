Chapter R2: Come With Us

The desmotarion is not a dramatic building. It is small, plain, near enough to the Agora that a man can hear the fishmongers from his cell if the wind is right. There is a bench, a window the size of a loaf of bread, and a hearth that smokes when it rains. I have been comfortable enough. People imagine prisons as theaters of suffering, but mine has been mostly a place to think, which is what I would have been doing anyway.

Crito arrived before the children. He is an old friend, and he had not slept. He had money — a great deal of money — and a plan, and a horse, and friends in Thessaly who would take me in. He had been talking for some time, very quickly, about all of this. I was listening as carefully as I could, which is to say I was watching his hands shake.

The door opened, and the four of them came in.

I had not seen them since they sailed for Corinth. They looked older. Pallas was carrying her flute. Klēta had her small hammer at her belt. Dolios had taken off his sandals out of respect for the place, which surprised me. Phoebe stood a little behind the others and watched, as she does.

"We have a boat," Klēta said, before anyone had even greeted me. "It is at Piraeus. The wind is good. We can be away before sundown."

Crito looked at the children as though they had been sent by the gods to confirm his argument. "You see?" he said to me. "Even they understand."

I asked them to sit. They sat. Crito remained standing, because he could not do otherwise.

"Tell me what you saw in Corinth," I said.

They told me. They told me about the ordered streets, the painted columns, the harmony of the proportions, the laws posted in the Agora where any citizen could read them. They told me about Pheidias and the workshops at Olympia. Klēta spoke for a long time about a system of weights she had seen, and about a court where two merchants had settled a dispute without anyone being killed. She had thought, watching it, that perhaps a city could be built well enough that people did not need to be wise. That the building itself would do the wisdom for them.

I asked her if she still thought so. She was quiet, then said she was not sure.

Pallas had been waiting. When her turn came, she did not argue. She took out the flute and played.

I closed my eyes. The music was the best she had ever made. There was Thermopylae in it, and the long walls, and the harbor at dawn, and something like the sound a mother makes when she does not want her child to know she is afraid. When she stopped, no one spoke for a while.

Then she said: "Leonidas stayed because his law required it. You should leave because your work is not finished. The cases are not the same. We need you. Come with us."

I sat down on the bench, though I had been sitting already. I mean that I sat down inside myself, the way I do when something has struck me and I need a moment.

"Pallas," I said. "Tell me again why the three hundred stayed."

"Because Spartan law required it."

"And was that virtue?"

"Yes."

"And if the law of Sparta had required them, instead, to march into a peaceful village and put every person in it into chains — would they have done it?"

She thought. "Yes. They would have."

"And would that have been virtue?"

She did not answer.

"The obedience would have been the same," I said. "The courage would have been the same. The discipline would have been the same. What would have been different?"

"The thing they were obeying," she said, very quietly.

"Yes. So courage is not the thing itself. Discipline is not the thing itself. Sacrifice is not the thing itself. There is something behind all of these, that decides whether they are noble or monstrous. Do you know what it is?"

"No."

"Neither do I," I said. "That is why I am still asking."

Crito made a small sound. I think he had forgotten I was the one on trial.

I told them then about Thucydides — the historian who had watched our long war with Sparta and written it all down, not as a song but as a diagnosis. He had set it before the city like a physician setting a wound before a patient: this is what happened to you, and this is why. The city had not read him. Or rather, the city had read him and had not changed. I told them about the Peace of Nicias, the treaty that had ended the war for a few years before collapsing — a clever arrangement, full of well-considered clauses, signed by serious men. It had not been wisdom. It had been a pause. Wisdom would have asked why we were fighting in the first place, and we had not wanted to ask that.

"So courage did not save us," I said. "And the treaty did not save us. And the laws — the laws I will not break by walking out that door tonight — the laws did not save us either. The laws are what I have. They are not what I trust."

"Then why stay?" Pallas asked.

"Because I have lived under them all my life and argued with them in the open, and a man who argues with the laws in the open and then sneaks away from them in the dark has not been honest. That is all. It is not a grand reason. I am sorry it is not grander."

Crito was weeping. I put my hand on his shoulder.

I looked at the four of them. "Go," I said. "Sail once more. Bring back whatever you find. I will be here."

They stood. Pallas put the flute away without looking at it. At the door, Phoebe turned and looked at me for a long moment, and then they were gone, and I could hear their footsteps in the corridor, and then I could not.
