Chapter 18: The Edge of the World

I should say at once that I did not see what the children saw next. I am reconstructing it from what they told me afterward, slowly, the way someone tells you about a dream they are still inside of.

They sailed east. The boat carried them, as it had carried them before, through both water and time, and they came to a flat country between two great rivers, far beyond anywhere a Greek of my generation would have imagined Greeks could go. The plain was called Gaugamela. The year, by their reckoning, was something like seventy years after my death.

There they watched a battle.

A young Macedonian — Alexander, son of Philip, who had not yet been born when the children first met me — stood with perhaps forty thousand men against a Persian king named Darius. Not the Darius our grandfathers fought at Marathon. A later Darius, the third of that name, but bearing the old name like an inherited cloak. He had perhaps two hundred thousand men. He had elephants, which Pallas had never seen. He had chariots with blades fitted to the wheels, designed to cut a man's legs from under him as they passed. He had chosen the ground himself — flat, open, room enough for his cavalry to swing around the Macedonian flanks like a closing hand.

Pallas told me that Alexander did not look at the numbers. He looked at the formation. He saw, somehow, where Darius was standing in his great line, and he charged not at the strongest point but obliquely, drawing the Persian line to stretch and then thin and then tear. When the tear opened, Alexander rode through it straight at Darius himself. Darius fled. When the king flees, Pallas said, the army becomes a crowd, and a crowd is no longer an army.

This was the second time Alexander had broken Darius. The first had been two years earlier, at a place called Issus, on the coast where Asia meets the sea. After Issus the eastern Mediterranean had fallen to him. After Gaugamela, the rest fell. Persepolis, the Persian capital — burned. Darius — murdered by his own satraps as he ran, which is what tends to happen to kings who lose battles. The empire that had once sent ships against Athens, that had once seemed to my grandfather's generation as permanent as the mountains, simply ceased.

Dolios was beside himself. He told me later, in some embarrassment, that he had wept watching Alexander ride. Here, finally, was what he had been looking for through all his travels. Not a poet. Not a craftsman. Not even a lawgiver. A man whose vision was so large that it pulled the world into a new shape behind him. And the vision, Dolios insisted, was not merely conquest. Alexander married a Persian woman. He ordered his officers to marry Persian women. He wore Persian dress, which scandalized his Macedonians. He spoke of a single human race under a single wise rule — Greek thought, Persian administration, the whole inhabited world made one.

This is the grandest of all the hopes the children carried. I want you to feel how grand it is before I say anything else. It is the hope that the trouble in their century — the certainties that cannot hear each other — could be answered by something large enough to contain all of them. One civilization. One vision. One ruler wise enough.

The children followed him east. The boat showed them, in compressed form, what came next. Egypt, where he was made a god. Babylon, where he held court like a Persian king. Bactria. The Hindu Kush, which is a range of mountains higher than anything in Hellas. The Indus river. And then, on the far bank of a river called the Hyphasis, his soldiers stopped. They had been marching for eight years. They were tired. They wanted to see their wives. They refused to go further.

Alexander went into his tent for three days and would not come out. When he came out, he turned the army around.

Phoebe told me — and this is the part I want you to hold — that on the long journey home he wept. Not from grief at losing men. He wept because there were no more worlds to conquer. The world had an edge after all, and he had reached it, and the edge was not a kingdom but his own soldiers saying no.

He died in Babylon. He was thirty-two years old. Younger than Pallas's mother, Dolios told me, in a voice I did not entirely follow. There was a fever, and there was perhaps poison, and there was certainly too much wine, and within days of his death his generals were already drawing knives over the question of who would inherit what. The unified world lasted as long as the man who held it in his head. Without him, it became four kingdoms, then more, then the long miserable wars the children's histories call the Diadochi — the Successors. Each successor claimed to be carrying Alexander's dream. Each used the dream to justify what he wanted anyway.

The children stood in Babylon, in the room where Alexander had died, and Dolios sat down on the floor.

"It needed him," he said. "The whole thing. It only worked while he was alive to want it."

Phoebe said, very quietly, "And even while he was alive — what was it that he wanted everyone to agree on? What were the shared values, underneath?"

No one answered her. They had crossed half the world and arrived back at the question I had asked them on the steps at Piraeus.

They went down to the boat. They turned it, this time, toward home.
