Chapter 4: The Sea Between

I have not, in my life, sailed much. The army carried me by ship a few times when it needed to, and otherwise I have been content to stand on land and watch the sea do what the sea does. But the children's boat was a different matter. To stand on its deck as it cut northeast from the Isthmus, the wind coming from the wrong direction and the boat going where it pleased anyway, was to feel that some assumption I had carried all my life had quietly come loose.

We left Corinth at dawn. Pallas worked the ropes. Klēta watched the sail and called small adjustments to her sister, though I noticed that the boat seemed to make some adjustments of its own, the rigging shifting before any hand had touched it. I asked Klēta about this. She thought for a moment and said, "It knows where we're going." Then she added, more quietly, "Or it knows where we ought to go. I'm not sure which."

I sat down on a low bench at the stern and let the morning unfold.

To our left, the long shoulder of Attica slid past. To our right, after some hours, the island of Euboea rose — a great spine of land running parallel to the mainland, so close in places that you might believe a strong swimmer could cross. I told the children what I knew of it. That the strait between Euboea and the mainland is called the Euripus, and that the current there changes direction several times a day, which no philosopher has yet satisfactorily explained. That when the Persians came, almost a lifetime ago now, the Greeks fought them in those waters at Artemision while Leonidas was holding the pass at Thermopylae. That Euboea has been, in every generation, a place Athens needed and could not always keep.

Phoebe listened with her chin on her knees. Dolios was lying flat on the deck, looking up at the sail as though he were trying to work out its trick.

"Socrates," he said after a while, "how far does the sea go?"

I told him I was the wrong man to ask. But I told him what I had heard.

To the south, the Aegean is scattered with islands — more than two hundred, the sailors say, though I have never met anyone who has counted them. They lie like stepping stones between Greece and Asia, and the Greeks have lived on them and quarreled over them for as long as anyone can remember. The Athenian fleet has gone among those islands collecting tribute, in the name of a league that began as a defense against Persia and became, by slow degrees, something else. I will not pretend I am proud of all of it.

To the west — and here I had to gesture vaguely, because I had never seen what I was describing — lies the Ionian Sea, which separates Greece from Italy. And beyond Italy, past the place where the boot meets the toe, is Sicily. Greeks live there too. Syracuse is a Greek city, and so is Akragas, and a dozen others. Some of them are wealthier than Athens. There are Greeks I will never meet, speaking a Greek I would mostly understand, eating bread baked from wheat that grew in soil my fathers never walked.

Klēta said, "I didn't know the Greek world was that big."

"Neither did I," I said, "until I was old enough to be embarrassed by how small I had thought it was."

The wind shifted. The boat, without consultation, leaned into the new direction and kept its course.

I have not said much about Poseidon in this account, because Poseidon is one of those gods one does not need to talk about much when one is on his territory. He is simply there — in the color of the water, which on that morning was a blue I cannot describe except to say that it did not look like any blue I had seen on land; in the long, steady swells that lifted us and let us down again as though some great creature were breathing beneath us; in the way the wind, even when it came from where we did not want it, somehow carried us forward anyway. The children had met him as a storm, on the night they first arrived. Now they were meeting him in his quieter aspect. I think they preferred this one, and I cannot blame them.

For a long while no one spoke. There is a kind of silence that can settle on a small boat in open water that does not feel like the absence of speech. It feels like something more attentive than speech. We were four travelers and an old man, suspended between one shore and another, with the whole Greek world spread out around us, far larger than any of us could see at once.

After a time Phoebe said, without looking at me, "We're going back to Athens, aren't we."

I told her I thought we were.

"Will you be all right?" she asked.

I told her I did not know. Which was, as far as I could tell, the truth.

The boat sailed on.
