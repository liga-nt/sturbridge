Chapter F: What He Left Behind

I was not there.

I want that to be the first thing on the page, because it is the first thing I have to admit about everything that follows. Phaedo will tell you the same — I was ill. That is the word I have used since, and it is true, though it is not the whole truth. A body can be ill from many causes. Mine was ill because the city I had been born into, the city of Theseus and Solon and Pericles, the city whose long walls ran down from the Acropolis to the sea like the arms of a mother — that city had decided, by a vote, that the wisest man in it should drink poison. I could not stand. I could not eat. I lay in my room in the house above the Kerameikos and listened to the cart-wheels on the stones and could not make myself rise.

So I stayed home. For a day and a night and most of another day, I stayed home.

There was a fire in the hearth. My mother's slave had built it that morning and I had not noticed her do it. I noticed it now. The room had grown dark around the small bright center of it, and the center was steady, and it had been steady all the while I had been weeping, and it occurred to me — I do not know how else to say this — that Hestia was in the room. Not as Athena comes, in armor and counsel. Not as Apollo comes, in light. She comes the way a hearth comes: by being already there. She does not travel. She does not adventure. She keeps the still center of the house, and on the day I could not move, she was the one Olympian I could bear.

She did not speak. I want to be careful about what I claim. The fire did not form words. But I understood, sitting in front of it, that I was supposed to go to the prison. That what I needed was at the still center of the thing I was running from.

I went.

The cell was empty of him. Crito had taken the body. The cup was gone. The bench where he had sat was bare. The room still smelled of the hemlock — a green, vegetable smell, almost ordinary. On the floor by the bench were wax tablets, stacked, and on the top tablet, in his hand, was the beginning of a story about four children.

You have just read it. Or most of it. The last conversation was not on the tablets, because he could not write it and live it at the same time. So I have done what I can. I have asked Phaedo and Crito and Simmias and Cebes and Apollodorus, each of them, separately, what was said when the four children walked into the cell that final morning. Their accounts do not entirely agree. I have written what I believe to be most faithful to the man I knew. But you should know that I am writing through grief, and through absence, and through the small distortions that any account acquires when it passes from a mouth to an ear to a hand. This is the last lesson, and I think he meant it: we do not fully possess the thing we most need. We approach it.

They came in, the four of them, and they had nothing.

That is what each of his friends told me, in different words. Pallas had set her flute down on the bench. Klēta's hammer was at her belt but her hands were empty. Dolios's sandals were tied together and slung over his shoulder; he had not flown in. Phoebe still had the spyglass, but she was not looking through it.

"Well," Socrates said. "What did you find?"

Dolios spoke first. He had been the most certain, on the way out, that he would return with the answer. He had stood on the unfinished frieze at Knossos and seen what Daedalus had seen, and he had believed, for a while, that a great enough vision could lift a civilization the way wings lift a boy. He told Socrates about the frieze. He told him about the wings. He told him that the wings had not held.

"And so?" said Socrates.

"And so I do not know," said Dolios, "what saves a civilization. I thought I did. I had a whole speech. I was going to give it to you. I have left it on the boat."

Socrates laughed. They all said he laughed. He sat down on the bench — he had been standing — and he laughed the way an old man laughs when a child finally stops pretending.

"Good," he said. "That is the speech I wanted to hear."

Phoebe raised the spyglass then. Not at the window. At him. She had been told, by the god who gave it to her, that it showed truth, and she had spent four journeys looking through it for gods and heroes and answers. She looked through it now at Socrates and she saw — she told me this herself, weeks later, when the children came to find me before they sailed — an old man on a bench in a cell, with bare feet, who did not know what came next and was not afraid.

She lowered the glass. She said, "There is nothing more it can show me than that."

"Then it has shown you everything," he said.

Pallas asked him if he was frightened. Crito said her voice broke when she asked it. Socrates considered the question the way he considered every question, as if he had never heard it before.

"I do not know what death is," he said. "If it is a long sleep without dreams, then it is a great gift, for I have not slept well in some years. If it is a journey to another place, where I might speak with Homer and with Hesiod and with the men who fought at Troy, then it is a greater gift still. I do not know which it is. I have noticed that the people who are most certain about it are the people who have been there least. So I will go and find out, and I will not pretend, on the way, to know what I am going toward."

Klēta asked if that was not terrifying — to walk toward a thing without knowing it.

"I used to think so," he said. "I have come to think the opposite. To walk toward a thing without pretending to know it is the only honest way to walk anywhere. The terror is in the pretending. When you set the pretending down, what is left is light enough to carry."

Then Crito brought the cup, and they wept, and he scolded them gently for weeping, and he drank, and he walked until his legs would not walk, and he lay down. The cold moved up from his feet. He spoke with Simmias and Cebes about the soul, and whether it was the kind of thing that could be unmade, and he did not pretend to settle it. At the end he uncovered his face and said to Crito, "We owe a cock to Asclepius. Pay the debt; do not forget." Asclepius is the god of healing. I have thought about that sentence for two years now and I am not finished thinking about it.

Then he died.

I have written down everything I could find of him since. I have written the conversation on the steps of the King Archon's court. I have written the speech he gave to the jury. I have written the night in the cell when Crito came with the escape plan, and the morning of the hemlock, and a great many other conversations besides — some I witnessed, some I did not, some I have arranged from fragments and some I have, I confess, imagined into a shape I think he would recognize. People will ask, after I am gone, which of the words in those dialogues are his and which are mine. I do not always know the answer. I have tried to be faithful. I am writing through grief, and through absence, and through a hearth-fire in a room above the Kerameikos that one evening became a goddess and sent me where I needed to go.

The children sailed. I watched their small boat from the long walls, doing the impossible thing Klēta had once explained to him on a coil of rope at Piraeus — going against the wind. I do not know if they reached their own century. I do not know if they saved it. I do not know what they told their people when they arrived, or whether their people listened.

I know only this. They came to Athens looking for an answer, and they left with a question, and the old man who gave them the question is dead, and I have been writing ever since, trying to finish the story they started, knowing I cannot finish it, knowing that this — this not-finishing — is the shape the story was always going to have.

He would have liked that, I think.

He would have sat down on a coil of rope and laughed.
