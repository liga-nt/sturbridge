Chapter 12: Know Thyself

We did not stay long in Athens after Olympia. The children were quiet on the road. Pallas walked ahead, Klēta walked beside me, and the boys came behind, occasionally arguing about something I couldn't hear and didn't try to. We took the road north into Boeotia, that flat and fertile plain where the wheat stands so thick in summer that you can lose a child in it. Demeter is the goddess they thank in those parts, and rightly. The Boeotians eat well. Athenians make jokes about how much they eat. I have never found this to be a mark against them.

Thebes rose ahead of us — a city on a low rise called the Cadmeia, after Cadmus who founded it long ago by sowing dragon's teeth in the earth and watching armed men spring up from the furrows. Most cities have founding stories. Thebes has one of the more troubling ones.

"It looks ordinary," Klēta said, shading her eyes.

I told her that Thebes was ordinary in many ways. Not as brilliant as Athens, not as severe as Sparta. A middling city, which is not an insult but a description. They had sided with the Persians during the great invasion, which Athenians had not yet stopped reminding them about and might never stop. They were prosperous farmers, decent soldiers. Their walls were strong. Their gods were the gods every Greek knows.

"And their stories?" Phoebe asked.

Their stories, I said, are the worst in Greece.

We entered through the southern gate and found a garden where an old man was willing to let us sit among his fig trees in exchange for news of Athens. I gave him what I could. Then the children asked me, as I knew they would, about Oedipus.

I sat down on a low wall. The wheat was visible through a gap between the houses, gold against the green of the hills. Somewhere a cart creaked. I told them the story the way Sophocles tells it — Sophocles, who is still alive in Athens as I speak, an old man writing plays I will never see finished, the greatest of our tragedians and the one who has thought hardest about Thebes.

A king and queen of this city, I said, were told by an oracle that their son would kill his father and marry his mother. So they pierced the infant's ankles and gave him to a shepherd to expose on a mountainside. The shepherd, being a person and not an instrument, could not do it. The child was passed to another shepherd and grew up in Corinth, a prince in a household not his own, knowing nothing of where he came from.

When he was a young man he heard the prophecy himself and fled Corinth to escape it, fled the parents he believed were his. On the road he quarreled with an old man at a crossroads and killed him. He came to Thebes and found the city tormented by a monster — the Sphinx, who asked travelers a riddle and devoured those who could not answer.

"What walks on four legs in the morning," Klēta said softly, "two at noon, and three in the evening."

So you know it, I said.

"Everyone knows it. Man. A baby crawls, a grown person walks, an old person uses a stick."

That is the answer, I said. Oedipus gave it. The Sphinx threw herself from the cliff and died. Thebes made him their king. He married the widowed queen, who was — as he eventually discovered — his mother. The old man at the crossroads had been his father.

The children were quiet.

"He answered the riddle," Klēta said. "He knew what humanity is. Four legs, two legs, three legs. He saw the whole shape of a human life."

He did, I said.

"But he didn't know himself."

He did not.

Klēta looked at me with the particular frown she wears when something does not fit together and she suspects the problem is in the materials. "At Delphi," she said, "above Apollo's temple — γνῶθι σεαυτόν. Know thyself. We saw it."

We did.

"That's what your god told you to do. That's the whole point."

It is, I said, what I have spent perhaps thirty years attempting to obey, with what I would call modest success.

"Then Oedipus —" she began, and stopped.

Oedipus, I said, was extraordinarily intelligent. There is no question of this. He saw what the wisest of Thebes could not see. He answered the question about humanity itself, and answered it correctly. And it did not save him. It did not even slow the disaster down. He went on solving — that is the terrible part — he went on solving puzzles right up to the moment he understood that he himself was the puzzle, and the answer was unbearable.

Pallas said, "What happened to him?"

He blinded himself, I said, and walked out of the city as a beggar, and at the end of his life came to a grove outside Athens called Colonus, and there the earth opened and Hades received him. Hades is not cruel, you understand. He is the god who holds what cannot be returned. Oedipus's mother was already with him. His father was already with him. His daughters would come in time. Hades simply keeps.

Klēta was looking at the wheat. "I thought," she said slowly, "that if our people could really know ourselves — what we are, what we're good at, what we keep getting wrong — we could fix it. I thought that was the answer to everything."

I said nothing. There are moments when a teacher should be a wall and not a door.

"Oedipus knew what humanity was," she said. "He still didn't know himself."

Yes.

"Then what is it to know yourself? If knowing isn't enough?"

I looked at her. The sun was beginning to slant. Somewhere in the fields, Demeter's wheat was bending in a wind I could not feel from the garden.

I do not know, I said. I have asked the question for thirty years. I will tell you what I have found if you wish, but I warn you — what I have found is mostly the shape of what I have not found.

"Tell us in Athens," Phoebe said quietly. "When we go back."

We will be back soon, I said. And we sat in the garden a while longer, among the figs, with the cursed city behind us and the green plain in front, and no one spoke, and it was not an uncomfortable silence. It was the silence of four children setting down something heavy and beginning, only beginning, to notice how tired their arms were.
