Chapter 9: Everything Beautiful

I should explain that I had not climbed the Acropolis in some months. A man on his way to trial finds his feet take him to other places — to old friends, to the Agora, to the steps of porches where he can argue about things that matter more than his own neck. But the children wanted to see the temples, and they were paying for the trip with their company, so up we went.

The road climbs steeply. Pallas took it like a soldier. Dolios took it like a goat. Phoebe took it the way she took most things — slowly, looking. Klēta lagged behind. She kept stopping to examine the joints in the retaining walls, running her fingers along the seams where one stone met another with no mortar between them. "How," she kept saying. "How did they."

At the top, the four of them stopped speaking for a long time.

I will tell you what they were seeing, because what visitors see now, in your time — if my guess is right about when you are reading this — is not what stood there then. The Parthenon was not white. The marble was white beneath, yes, but the figures along the frieze were painted: deep blue for the backgrounds, vermilion red for the cloaks, gold for the hair of gods. The pediment groups blazed. Athena Promachos, the great bronze, stood spear-up and helmeted, and her spear-point caught the sun so brightly that sailors rounding Cape Sounion used it to find home. Beside the Parthenon stood the Erechtheion, with its porch of women — six maidens of stone holding the roof on their heads as patiently as women hold up most things. The little Temple of Athena Nike perched at the edge of the cliff like a bird about to leap.

Phoebe lifted her spyglass. I had grown used to her doing this. She lowered it and her eyes were wet. "It's even more than I can see with my eyes," she said. "It's — it's all painted. It's all on fire with color."

"Pericles built this," said Pallas, with some reverence. She had been reading her history.

"Yes," I said. "Pericles built this."

I said it carefully, because I have complicated feelings about Pericles, who was a great man in the way great men are great — meaning that some of what he did was magnificent and some of what he did I cannot defend. He took the treasury of the Delian League — the money our allies paid in to defend Greece against Persia — and he spent a great deal of it here, on this hill, on these temples. Our allies were not always pleased. Some of them were forced to remain in the league when they wished to leave. The marble you are looking at, I might have said to the children, was paid for in part by cities that did not want to pay for it. But I did not say this yet. I let them look first. It seemed only fair.

Dolios was already imagining what could be built if his people had this kind of vision. I could see it in his face. Pallas was thinking about what such beauty was worth defending. Phoebe was praying — she does this without announcing it.

But Klēta had walked away.

I found her at the edge of the Acropolis, looking down. She was not looking at the Parthenon. She was looking at the Agora below, at a smaller temple set among the smoke of the bronze-workers' shops. The Hephaistion. I had passed it a thousand times. The god of the forge had his house there, surrounded by the men who worked metal, the men whose hands were always burned and whose aprons were always black. It was a fitting arrangement. Hephaestus does not live where things are admired. He lives where things are made.

"Can we go down there?" she said.

So we went down. Pallas grumbled — she had wanted more time on the heights — but Klēta would not be moved, and the rest of us followed because Klēta had not asked for anything our whole journey, and when the quiet ones ask, you go.

The Hephaistion is a smaller temple, not as ambitious as what stands above it, but well-made. Every joint true. Every column the same as every other column. Klēta walked up to it slowly. She had brought from the boat the small iron hammer she used for everything — fixing a cleat, prying a knot loose, tapping in a pin. I had seen her use it a hundred times without thinking about it.

She knelt at the threshold and laid the hammer flat against the stone.

I cannot tell you what she felt. She told me later, and I will tell you what she told me, but I was not inside her hands. She said the stone was warm. She said it hummed. She said she could feel — not hear, feel — the heat of a forge that was not there, the ringing of metal being struck, the moment when a thing that has never existed begins to exist under someone's hands. She said the god was in the stone. Not speaking. Just there, the way a master craftsman is there in his workshop even when his back is turned.

She stayed kneeling for a long time. When she stood up, she was holding the hammer differently — as though she had only just understood what it was.

"This was his," she said quietly to me. "Wasn't it. From the beginning. He gave it to me before I knew."

I sat down on the temple steps. I did this often on this journey. I told her I did not know what gods give to whom, but that the hammer had been doing useful work in her hands the entire time I had known her, and perhaps that was the only proof one needed.

Above us, far up on the rock, the Parthenon blazed in its painted glory, paid for in part by people who had not wished to pay. And below us, in the smoke of the workshops, a girl held a small hammer and felt that making things might be the thing that saved the world.

I did not tell her, yet, what I was thinking. There would be time for questions. There always was.
