Chapter R3: I Will Be Here

They unchained me that morning. The iron came off my ankle and I rubbed the place where it had been, which is a small pleasure I recommend to anyone who has worn chains.

Simmias and Cebes were already there, and Crito, and several others whose names you would know if I listed them. We had been speaking about the soul — whether it persists, whether it goes somewhere, whether the question even makes sense. I was enjoying myself. Cebes had just made a clever objection and I was settling in to answer it when I heard footsteps in the corridor and looked up to see four children at the door of my cell.

The guard, who by now knew them, let them pass without a word.

The hearth fire was burning low. I have always thought of Hestia as the most patient of the gods — while the others go racing off to wars and love affairs, she stays at the hearth, keeping the fire alive so there is something to come home to. The prison had a small fire in a clay vessel, and that fire was hers, and it lit the faces of my friends and the four children equally.

"Sit," I said. "We were speaking of the soul, but the soul will wait."

Klēta would not sit. She had the look of someone who has been rehearsing on the road from Piraeus and cannot let the speech cool before delivering it. So I gestured for her to begin.

She had been to the Parthenon, she said, and stood beneath its columns, and seen how every line that looks straight is in fact slightly curved so that the eye perceives it as straight — a correction for the failures of human sight. She had been to the theater of Dionysus and watched the Oresteia, in which a chain of murders is finally broken not by another murder but by a court — by twelve citizens voting. She had been to Thebes, where the Sphinx asked her riddle and was answered, and the city was freed by a man who simply thought clearly. She had seen, she said, that human beings make things. They make temples and plays and courts and answers to riddles. And when they make beautiful things, the beautiful things hold the city together.

"This is what we have lost," she said. "We stopped making. We let our hands go idle and our institutions rot. If we go home and build again — beautifully, carefully — we can heal."

I sat down on the bench by the hearth. Klēta watched me sit, and her face was very serious, because she had learned by now what my sitting meant.

"Klēta," I said. "When you stood beneath the Parthenon — was the beauty in the stone, or in the men who cut it?"

"In the men," she said. "The stone is just stone."

"And the men who cut the stone — were their souls beautiful?"

She thought. "I think so. To make something like that, you would have to be."

"Klēta," I said, "the city that built the Parthenon is the city that is killing me this afternoon. The same Assembly that voted for those columns voted for this hemlock. The same hands."

She opened her mouth and closed it.

"I am not saying the Parthenon is ugly," I said. "I am asking whether a city can make beautiful things and still have an ugly soul. And if it can — then the beautiful things are not what holds the city together, are they? Something else does. Or something else fails to."

She sat down on the floor by the hearth. She did not cry. She just sat, the way a person sits when a load they have been carrying turns out to weigh nothing, and they discover they have been straining against air.

"I don't know what holds it together," she said.

"Neither do I," I said. "But that is a much better place to begin than where you began an hour ago."

Phoebe had been quiet through all of this. Now she lifted her spyglass — the one she had carried since the beginning, the one that was supposed to show her truth — and she pointed it not at the window, not at the corridor, not at anything outside the cell.

She pointed it at me.

She held it there for a long time. I did not ask what she saw. A man does not ask such things of a child with a spyglass.

When she lowered it, her eyes were wet, but she was smiling — the small, surprised smile of someone who has finally found what they were sent to find and discovered it was much closer than they had thought.

"What did you see?" Pallas asked her.

"An old man," Phoebe said. "Just an old man. He doesn't know anything and he isn't afraid."

I laughed at that, because it was accurate, and accuracy in a thirteen-year-old is a rare and precious thing.

"There is one more journey," I said. "I want you to make it. Go down to the boat and sail wherever the wind takes you, and come back when you are ready."

"Will you be here?" Dolios asked.

I looked at the hearth, where Hestia's fire was still burning quietly, and at my friends, who would weep later but were not weeping yet, and at the cup that I had not yet been given.

"I will be here when you return," I said. "Though by then I may be writing rather than speaking. You will know me by the words on the page."

They did not understand. That was all right. Plato, who was not in the room that morning — he had taken ill, and stayed home, and would always be sorry for it — Plato would understand later, when he found what I left behind.

The children went down to the harbor. The fire kept burning. We returned, my friends and I, to the question of the soul.
