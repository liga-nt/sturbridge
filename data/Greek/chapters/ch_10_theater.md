Chapter 10: What the Audience Felt

We climbed from the Agora up the southern slope of the Acropolis, and the children gasped — which is the correct response to the Theater of Dionysus the first time you see it.

Seventeen thousand seats curve into the hillside like a great stone bowl. At the bottom is a circle of beaten earth — the orchestra — and at its center stands the altar of Dionysus, the thymele. Around the rim of the orchestra, in front of the first row, sit the proedria: marble seats for priests and dignitaries. The throne of the priest of Dionysus is in the middle, carved with figures so fine you would think Pheidias himself had chiseled them between commissions.

It was the second day of the Dionysia. The whole city was present. Athens does not consider theater an amusement. Athens considers it a mirror. On festival days, courts close, business stops, and the citizens sit together in the open air and watch their own souls played back to them.

"Why is the altar in the middle of the stage?" Klēta asked.

I told her that Dionysus is the god of wine and of dissolution — the loosening of the self. To act in a play, a man must dissolve himself and become someone else. To watch a play, an audience must dissolve themselves and become the crowd inside the story. Theater is therefore Dionysus's province. He stands at the center because he is what permits the whole strange business to happen.

The play that day was by Sophocles, who in his long life was never placed lower than second in the competitions. Before him there had been Aeschylus — the father of tragedy, who fought at Marathon and wrote the Oresteia, the great trilogy of Agamemnon's house, of blood for blood until at last the Furies are persuaded to become something gentler. After Sophocles would come Euripides, who put Medea on this stage with her terrible knife, and Hippolytus, broken by a goddess he did not honor. Three poets, three views of the human disaster. Athens loved them all and gave them prizes and quarreled about which was greatest, the way Athens quarrels about everything.

The chorus entered. Phoebe leaned forward and did not lean back for the rest of the afternoon.

I will not try to summarize what we saw. I will say only that at one point, when the messenger came out and described what had been discovered inside the palace, I heard seventeen thousand people draw breath at the same moment, and I understood again why this art is taken so seriously here. Phoebe's face was wet. She did not know it was wet.

Between episodes, while the chorus sang, Pallas leaned over to me. "Tell them about Arachne," she whispered. She had heard the story somewhere on her travels and wanted the others to have it.

So I told it quietly, beneath the music. There was once a girl named Arachne, the finest weaver in Lydia, whose tapestries were so beautiful that the nymphs left their streams to watch her work. And because she was very gifted, she became very proud, and she said that her skill was greater than Athena's. The goddess came to her in the shape of an old woman and warned her to take it back. Arachne refused. So they had a contest. Athena wove the gods in their dignity. Arachne wove the gods in their cruelty — every kidnapping, every betrayal, every lie — and her weaving was, in truth, perfect. Athena could find no flaw in it. And that, you understand, was the trouble. Athena struck her, and Arachne in shame tried to hang herself, and the goddess in pity turned her into a spider, so that she might weave forever, but only webs.

"That's a terrible story," said Dolios.

"It is a story about craft without humility," I said. "Arachne could make anything. She could not make herself wise."

The play resumed. At the end, when the blinded king was led offstage and the chorus spoke its final lines, the audience did not applaud immediately. They sat. Even the loud ones sat.

Walking down afterward, Phoebe could hardly speak. Then she said, "Socrates. If everyone in our city saw a play like that — really saw it — they would have to change. They would feel it, and they would have to change."

I did not answer her at once. I was looking at the stone seats emptying around us, at the priest of Dionysus rising from his throne, at the altar in the middle of the orchestra where the god had presided over what we had just felt.

I said, "Phoebe, this same audience watched the Oresteia. They watched Oedipus. They wept where you wept. And then some of them went home, and the next year voted to sail to the island of Melos and kill every man there and sell the women and children. The same people. The same eyes that had cried for Hecuba."

She looked at me as though I had hit her.

"I am not saying the plays are nothing," I said quickly. "I am saying that feeling a true thing is not the same as living by it. Tears are not yet a life."

We walked a little further. I cleared my throat. "I should tell you, in the interest of fairness, that this same theater also stages comedies. A few years ago a clever young man named Aristophanes put on a play called The Clouds. In it there is a foolish old philosopher hung up in a basket, talking nonsense about gnats. The character's name was Socrates."

Dolios stared at me. "Did you go?"

"I went," I said. "I stood up so the audience could see I was the one being mocked. They laughed harder."

"That's awful," said Klēta.

"It was very funny, actually," I said. "He had me exactly. That is the trouble with good writers. They see you."

Phoebe was quiet. She was holding the play still, inside her, as one holds water in cupped hands.

I let her hold it. The aporia would come later, and there was no need to hurry it.
