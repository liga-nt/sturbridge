Chapter P: The Wisest Man in Athens

When I was living in fifth-century Athens, four children appeared in the Agora on the day I was due in court.

I should explain about the court. The King Archon had received charges against me — impiety, corrupting the youth, the usual list of complaints brought by men who cannot quite say what is bothering them and so reach for whatever words sound serious. I was on my way to answer those charges. This being Athens, I was not hurrying. I paused to watch the fishmongers argue and to listen to a general explain a battle he won — though I noticed that the details of the victory grew more and more impressive as the the story wore on. I heard politicians making promises they understood no better than the people who believed them. Above us all, the Parthenon caught the morning light, looking as splendid as Pericles had intended when he built it in the great years of the empire, before the long war with Sparta, before the plague, before everything the city had once been certain of had been quietly taken away.

It was in the middle of all this that four children appeared.

They did not appear out of thin air — I want to be clear about that, because people who heard the story later added that detail, as people always add details that seem as though they ought to be true. They walked up from the harbor like any visitor to the city. What was unusual was not how they arrived but how they looked: like people who had just set something very heavy down and were only beginning to notice how tired their arms were.

The oldest girl caught sight of me first. She was perhaps thirteen, dark-eyed, the kind of child who watches before she speaks. "You are Socrates," she said. It was not a question.

This is what people call me, I said, though I had never been entirely certain it was the most accurate description.

She stared at me. "We are not from Athens," she said. "We are not from the fifth century."

Well... I have encountered many unusual arguments in the Agora. I assumed these children had been listening to one of the Sophists, cleaver teachers who make wonderful-sounding arguments that turn to smoke when you try to touch them.

"From the future," I said. "Is that so?"

"Yes," said the boy — younger, quick-eyed, wearing a small grin. I could tell he was accustomed to charming his way through difficult conversations. "We know you probably don't believe us."

I told him that I did not yet disbelieve them, which is a different matter.

I would have left it at that, but the youngest girl, Klēta — ten years old, with eyes as sharp a carving knife — offered me something quite tangible, "Our boat is down at Piraeus, if you want to see it."

So we walked down to the harbor. A man on his way to his trial does not refuse a child who wants to show him a boat.

What I saw at the dock made me sit down an a coil of rope. The vessel was small. The sail was set on a pivot the likes of which I had never seen. Ropes ran in complex patterns as though Hephaistos had laid them down to capture Ares again. I was totally bewildered. Klēta saw my confusion and explained patiently – the way one explains to an old man — that with this rigging the boat could sail at an angle into the wind. Not with the wind. Against it.

I asked her to repeat that.

She repeated it.

I sat for some time. I thought about Agamemnon's fleet at Aulis, becalmed and contrary-winded, unable to sail for Troy, and about Iphigeneia. If the Greeks at Aulis had owned a rope-pattern like this child's... I am not often silenced, but it took me some time to take this all in. When I finally spoke, I did so with a seriousness equal to the gravity of what I was witnessing.

"Children," I said, "tell me what kind of trouble you and your people are in."

They told me. I will not reproduce everything they said because some of it I did not fully understand. But the shape of their difficulty was familiar to me. Their time was full of people who were absolutely certain they were right. Different people, certain of different things, each group convinced that its certainty was the correct one. Because everyone was certain, no one was listening. Because no one was listening, the strife was growing.

"And you have come to me," I said, "for the answer."

"Yes," said the decisive one. Pallas, they called her. "We came to you because you are the wisest man in Athens. The oracle said so. And we need your wisdom"

At this I frowned and looked around for a way to escape. You see, I was afraid these children were going to grow angry at me like my accusers when the learned I couldn't give them what they were seeking. They must have read my thoughts because they closed in around me and I could tell they wouldn't let me escape. So I told them what the oracle actually meant — or what I had come to suspect it meant after some years of investigation. You see, the god had not said I was wise. The god had said no one in Athens was wiser than Socrates. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole matter. For I had gone to people who were supposed to know things — generals, poets, craftsmen, politicians — and I had found, every time, that they did not know half the things they thought they knew. It wasn't that I knew more than them but that I was quite clear on the fact that I knew nothing. That small, embarrassing piece of honesty was apparently the only wisdom in the city.

When I finished my explanation, the kids' faces were like the sea below the Pireaeus, when the winds are fighting on the surface and the spray is rushing this way and that.

"So you don't have the answer," said the Dolios.

"I have no answer," I said, "In fact, I own almost nothing at all. But tell me — the thing you are trying to save — what is it? What is the virtue your people once had but have now lost? For surely a society that can build ships that sail against the wind have also a moral technology that allows them to navigate the storms of social strife"

All the kids began to answer at once. And within the hour they had contradicted themselves more times than I could count. In the end, their arguments trailed off. 

Phoebe, the watchful one, finally said: "We don't actually know what it is, do we?"

"Probably not." I said.

I didn't want to disappoint the children so I told them what I could offer. I was an old man. I was due in court within the hour, on charges I knew I could not flatter by taking seriously. I owned nothing and knew nothing of use. But I could perhaps be a companion in their confusion and help the consider what question to ask next.

They considered this. Then Klēta said: "We have our boat. If you can't help us. Where should we go next?"

I smiled. "Delphi," I said. "Everyone who wants answers goes to Delphi." 

I turned back toward the city and made my way slowly up the long walls until I reached the Areopagus, a bare limestone outcrop just south of the agora. There I turned back and saw the children's tiny bark doing just what they told me it could do, sailing just 45 degree off the wind. And said a prayer to the god for their safety, quite certain and equally mistaken, that I would never see them again.
