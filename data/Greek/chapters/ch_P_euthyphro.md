The Wisest Man in Athens

When I was living in fifth-century Athens, four children appeared in the Agora on the day I was due in court.

I should explain about the court. The King Archon had received charges against me — impiety, corrupting the youth, the usual list of complaints brought by men who cannot quite say what is bothering them and so reach for whatever words sound serious. I was on my way to answer those charges. This being Athens, I was not hurrying. I paused to watch the fishmongers argue and to listen to a general explain a battle he won — though I noticed that the details of the victory grew more and more impressive as the the story wore on. I heard politicians making promises they understood no better than the people who believed them. Above us all, the Parthenon caught the morning light, looking as splendid as Pericles had intended when he built it in the great years of the empire, before the long war with Sparta, before the plague, before everything the city had once been certain of had quietly worn away, like the stone columns a temple that the people no longer visit.

It was in the middle of all this that four children appeared.

They did not appear out of thin air — I want to be clear about that, because people who heard the story later added that detail, as people always add details that they feel will attract the crowd. They walked up from the harbor like any visitor to the city. What was unusual was not how they arrived but how they looked: like people who had just set something very heavy down and were only beginning to notice how tired their arms were.

The oldest girl caught sight of me first. She was perhaps thirteen, dark-eyed, the kind of child who watches before she speaks. "You are Socrates," she said. It was not a question.

This is what people call me, I said, though I had never been entirely certain it was the most accurate description.

She stared at me. "We are not from Athens," she said. "We are not from the fifth century."

Well... I have encountered many unusual arguments in the Agora. I assumed these children had been listening to one of the Sophists, cleaver teachers who make wonderful-sounding arguments that turn to smoke when you try to touch them.

"From the future," I said. "Is that so?"

"Yes," said the boy — younger, quick-eyed, wearing a small grin. I could tell he was accustomed to charming his way through difficult situations. "We know you probably don't believe us, but look, we have a book!"

I was not charmed by this, but I should explain why. I have spent a long life distrusting the written word — not because writing is useless, but because it creates a dangerous semblance of knowledge. A written argument looks the same whether it is true or false. It cannot answer your questions. It sits there, patient and immovable, and says the same thing to the wise man and the fool alike. I have watched students memorize speeches and deliver them with great force, only to learn that they understood nothing about the subject of the oration. So when a charming boy waves a book at me as proof of anything, I am, if anything, more skeptical than I was before.

And yet, I must confess that this book was unlike any I had encountered in the libraries and book stalls of Athens. It was bound and flat, with a stiff cover, of a kind I had never seen. The pages were as smooth as the Aegean on a hot and windless day and the paintings, if that's what they were, were of a remarkable quality, as though the figures were ready to come to life right off the page. Klēta opened it to a page near the middle and handed it to me. There was an illustration of the Acropolis. I recognized it immediately — there was the Parthenon, the Propylaea, the statue of Athena whose spear tip sailors use to navigate into Piraeus. But surrounding the illustration was text in a language I did not understand. When I asked what it meant, Kleta read it to me. The Parthenon, it said, was built between 447 and 432 BCE. I did not know what BCE meant.

I turned a few pages and found a section on the Persian Wars. I found a map of the Aegean that was better than any map I had ever seen. I found, somewhat further along, a brief mention of my own trial. When we reached that point, Dolios leapt forward and closed the book. That part isn't very interesting he said, but the warmth of his smile was in stark contrast to the chill that had seemed to take hold of him.

It's okay, I told him, I'm not one to lose my head over a book. 

It was growing late and I was starting to make my excuses when the youngest girl Klēta — ten years old, with eyes as sharp a carving knife — offered me something much more tangible than the written word, "Our boat is down at Piraeus, if you want to see it."

So we walked down to the harbor. For all my distrust of writing, I considered boats about the most honest thing on earth. If an argument fails, the arguer usually denies it. But if a boat fails, it sinks to the bottom of the sea and no one is deceived about whether it was a worthy craft. 

What I saw at the dock made me sit down an a large coil of abandoned rope. The vessel was small. The sail was set on a pivot the likes of which I had never seen. Smooth ropes ran in complex patterns as though Hephaistos had laid them down to once more capture Ares. I was totally bewildered. Klēta saw my confusion and explained patiently – the way a child explains to an old man — that with this rigging the boat could sail at an angle into the wind. Not with the wind. Against it.

I asked her to repeat that.

She repeated it.

I sat for some time. I thought about Agamemnon’s fleet at Aulis, becalmed and contrary-winded, unable to sail for Troy, and about Iphigenia. If the Greeks at Aulis had owned a rope-pattern like this child’s… it was almost too much to take in. You see, in the Athens of my day, when a captain must cross the Aegean and the wind is at his front, he waits. Sometimes he waits for days. Sometimes he waits so long that armies grow restless and the minds of kings go dark with their own desire. When I was twelve years old, I sat in the theater of Dionysus and watched Aeschylus premiere the Oresteia — three tragedies that trace the curse on the house of Agamemnon from its root to its resolution. The root is Aulis. Agamemnon sacrifices his daughter Iphigenia to obtain a favorable wind, sails to Troy, and wins. He returns home to find his wife Clytemnestra waiting — not with garlands but with her own murderous plan. She kills him to avenge her daughter. But her son Orestes, raised in exile, comes back to Argos to avenge his father, and kills her. The Furies pursue him to Athens, where Athena convenes the first murder trial at the Areopagus and breaks the chain of blood vengeance with a jury’s verdict. I was a boy, and it shook me. Not because the story was terrible — though it was — but because of what Aeschylus was saying underneath it: that the court, with its procedures and its civic patience, is what civilization offers in place of the feud. It struck me as the most serious idea I had ever encountered. It still does. It was only fitting that I should be reminded of all this by children the god had sent to find me as I was climbing the steps of the king's court to answer my own grave charges.

When I finally spoke, I did so with a seriousness equal to the gravity of this children's revelation.

"Children," I said, "tell me what kind of trouble you and your people are in."

They told me. I will not recount everything they said because some of it I did not fully understand. But the shape of their difficulty was familiar to me. Their time was full of people who were absolutely certain they were right. Different people, certain of different things, each group convinced that its certainty was the correct one. Because everyone was certain, no one was listening. Because no one was listening, the strife among their people was growing.

"And you have come to me," I said, "for the answer."

"Yes," said the decisive one. Pallas, they called her. "We came to you because you are the wisest man in Athens. The oracle said so. And we are certain we need your wisdom."

At this I frowned and looked around for a way to escape. You see, I was afraid these children would grow angry at me like my accusers when the learned I couldn't give them what they were seeking. They must have read my thoughts because they closed in around me and wouldn't let me escape. So I told them what the oracle actually meant — or what I had come to suspect it meant after some years of investigation. You see, the god had not said I was wise. The god had said no one in Athens was wiser. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole matter. For after receiving the oracle's message, I had gone to people who were supposed to know things — generals, poets, craftsmen, politicians — and had found that, to a person, they did not know half the things they thought they knew. It wasn't that I knew more than a general about leading armies or a sculptor about carving statues, but while they rode their expertise in one domain to certainty about many other things – like Poseison upon his charriot – I was quite clear that I did not know what I did not know. That small, embarrassing piece of honesty was apparently the only wisdom in our illustrious city.

When I finished my explanation, the kids' faces were like the sea below the Pireaeus, when the winds are fighting on the surface and the spray is rushing this way and that.

"So you don't have the answer?" said Dolios.

"I'm afraid not," I said, "but if you want further evidence of my ineptitude, at more than 80 years old, I have almost nothing to my name and I face charges that may end in my total demise. But tell me what is it that you are trying to save in your city? What is the virtue your people once had but have now lost? For surely a society that sail against the wind has also a moral or political techne that allows them to navigate the storms of social strife."

All the kids began to answer at once. And within the hour they had contradicted themselves more times than I could count. In the end, their arguments trailed off. 

Phoebe, the watchful one, finally said: "Seems we know about as much as you do about our problem."

I smiled timidly, afraid they would blame me for their new found confusion. I didn't want to disappoint these children who seemed to me unusually sincere, so I told them what I could offer. I was an old man. I was due in court within the hour, on charges I knew I could not flatter by taking seriously. I owned nothing and knew nothing of use. But I could perhaps join them in their confusion and help them consider what question to ask next.

They considered this. Then Klēta said: "We have our boat. If you can't help us. Where should we go next?"

I smiled. "Delphi," I said. "Everyone who wants answers goes to Delphi." 

I turned back toward the city and made my way slowly up the long walls until I reached the Areopagus, a bare limestone outcrop just south of the agora. There I turned back and saw the children's tiny bark doing just what they told me it could do, sailing just 45 degree off the headwind. I said a prayer to the god for their safety, quite certain and equally mistaken, that I would never see them again.
