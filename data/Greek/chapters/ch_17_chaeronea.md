Chapter 17: The Lion of Chaeronea

I did not see Chaeronea with my own eyes. I will say that plainly. Chaeronea was fought thirty years after my death. But the children went there, and what they told me afterward I have set down as faithfully as I can, because some things deserve to be remembered even by those who could not be present.

The plain of Chaeronea is the kind of place a traveler would pass through without noticing. Flat fields, the green of late summer beginning to brown, low hills rising on either side like the rim of a shallow bowl. Boeotia. Farmland. The road from Thebes runs through it and on toward Phocis, and ordinarily nothing happens there except the harvest.

On this day, a king from the north had come down with an army.

His name was Philip, of Macedon, and the children had already heard a great deal about him before they arrived. They had stopped in Thebes on the way — a city I had visited often in my own time, full of stubborn farmers and excellent wrestlers and men who took their gods seriously. Thebes had been the leading power in Greece for some years. Now Thebes had marched out, with Athens beside her, to stop Philip from coming any farther south.

Pallas told me, when she finally spoke of it, that the noise was the worst part. She had thought a battle would sound like the songs make it sound — clear voices, clear orders. It did not. It sounded, she said, like a marketplace on fire.

I want to talk about Philip first, because Dolios wanted to talk about Philip first, and I have learned to begin where my interlocutors begin.

Philip of Macedon was, by every account the children gathered, the cleverest political mind of his generation. He had inherited a poor mountain kingdom that the Greeks of the south considered barely Greek at all. Within twenty years he had reformed its army, bought its enemies, married its rivals, and made himself the master of everything north of Thermopylae. He had done with cunning and patience what Xerxes could not do with a million men. He had united Greece.

Or — he was about to. Chaeronea was the day he finished the work.

Dolios watched the battle from a hillside, with the others, and what he watched was the Macedonian phalanx — longer spears than any Greek had carried, in tighter formation than any Greek had marched — grinding forward across the plain. He watched Philip's son, an eighteen-year-old boy named Alexander, lead the cavalry charge that broke the Theban line. And he watched the Sacred Band of Thebes refuse to break.

The Sacred Band were three hundred soldiers — one hundred and fifty pairs of lovers, sworn to each other and to the city. For a generation they had been the finest infantry in Greece. They held the right of the Theban line that day. When the line collapsed around them, they did not run. They closed ranks where they stood and fought until the last of them was dead.

Dolios told me this part very quietly. He had spent the whole journey, he said, admiring people who could talk their way out of trouble. He had wanted to be the cleverest person in every room. And here was Philip — the cleverest person in all of Greece — and what his cleverness had produced was a field of three hundred dead men who would not be talked out of anything.

After the battle, Philip walked the field. The children saw this. He came to where the Sacred Band lay, in their ranks, none of them with a wound in the back. And he stopped, and he wept. He said — Phoebe heard it through her spyglass, which catches some words across distances I cannot explain — "Perish miserably any who suspect these men of doing or suffering anything shameful."

He wept, and then he went on with the business of subjugating Greece.

He created what he called the League of Corinth. Every Greek city would be free, he said. Every Greek city would also do what Philip told it to do. The city-states, the small fierce communities in which men had argued in the agora and voted in the assembly and made fools of themselves in public for three centuries — these were finished. They would continue to exist. They would not continue to matter.

Dolios said to me, when they returned: "He stopped the wars, Socrates. He stopped the squabbling. Isn't that what we wanted?"

I asked him what Philip had stopped the squabbling for.

He could not answer. He said Philip had been killed two years later, by one of his own bodyguards, and that Alexander had taken the army east and conquered Persia and then died in Babylon at thirty-two, and that within a generation Alexander's empire had broken into pieces ruled by his quarreling generals.

I asked him whether the silence Philip had imposed had outlasted Philip.

He said it had not.

The Thebans, some years later, came back to that plain and built a stone lion over the grave of the Sacred Band. It faces north, toward the direction from which Philip came. It is still there, the children said. It is the only thing on that plain that anyone remembers.

I think the Thebans who built it knew what they were marking. Not a defeat — they had suffered defeats before. Something else. The end of the kind of world in which a man like me could stand in a marketplace and ask annoying questions of his fellow citizens, and have those citizens — free, self-governing, infuriating — answer him back.

Dolios sat with this for a long time before he spoke again.
