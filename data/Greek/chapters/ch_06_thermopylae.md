Chapter 6: Obedient to Their Laws

I have never been to Thermopylae in my own time. I went as an old man with four children in a boat that could sail against the wind, and we arrived in a year that had not yet happened to me — 480 BCE, the year my city's grandfathers were still fighting men. The boat had carried us not only down the coast but back through time, which I will admit I do not pretend to understand. I only describe what I saw.

The pass is called the Hot Gates because of the springs that bubble up from the rock, smelling of sulfur, steaming even in the warm afternoon. The cliffs rise on one side and the sea presses on the other, and between them is a strip of ground in places no wider than a cart. A man with sense looks at such a place and understands at once why an army would choose to die there rather than retreat from it.

We arrived on the morning of the second day of the battle. I will not describe what the children saw on the ground itself. I will say only that war, even a glorious war, is mostly an arrangement of bodies that were once young, and the smell of blood mixed with the sulfur of the springs is something a person does not forget, even when the person is as old as I am and has tried to forget many things.

High on the cliff above the eastern end of the pass, a canopy had been raised. Beneath it sat a man on a chair carried up by his slaves. This was Xerxes, King of Kings, ruler of an empire that stretched from the Indus to the Aegean. He was watching the battle the way a wealthy man at the theater watches a play he has paid for and is not enjoying. His invasion had come down through Thrace and Macedonia, gathering Greek cities as it came, and at this narrow place — and at this place only — it had stopped.

It had stopped because of three hundred Spartans and seven hundred Thespians, and a man named Leonidas.

Pallas saw him first. She had been quiet since we arrived, which was unlike her. Leonidas was standing at the front rank, a man of perhaps fifty with a gray-shot beard, in armor that had been mended many times. He was speaking with his officers. He was not shouting. He was not weeping. He was speaking the way a carpenter speaks to other carpenters about the work that needs doing before the day ends.

"He knows," Pallas said.

I asked her what she meant.

"He knows they're going to die. He knew this morning. He knows now. He's just — doing it."

I sat down on a stone near the springs. I was thinking about a great many things at once, and I have learned that when this happens it is best to sit. I thought about Ephialtes, the local man who had shown the Persians the mountain path around the pass. I thought about the herald who had brought Leonidas the order to retreat, which Leonidas had refused. I thought about the law of Sparta, which forbade a Spartan to abandon his post, and which Leonidas was at that moment obeying with such complete obedience that the obedience itself looked like a kind of love.

Klēta said quietly, "Why doesn't he run?"

"Because his laws say he can't," Pallas said. And there was something in her voice — a steadiness — that I had not heard in her before.

We watched until the sun grew low. The Spartans and the Thespians fought in the narrow place, and they killed many Persians, and they were killed themselves, slowly, as the day went on. Dolios turned away after a while and would not look. Phoebe watched everything without speaking. Klēta kept asking small practical questions about armor and formations, which I think was her way of not crying.

Pallas did not turn away. She watched Leonidas until he fell, and after he fell she watched the men who fought over his body to keep the Persians from taking it, and after that too she kept watching.

When the pass was quiet she walked away from us. I let her go. Some things a child must do alone, and an old man's company would only spoil it.

She came back after dark. She was carrying a flute. I do not know where she got it — she said she had found it among the rocks, as if someone had set it down and forgotten it — and I did not press her. The wood was old and the workmanship was finer than any I had seen. She held it the way one holds something that has chosen its owner.

"I played it," she said. "Up there. At the pass."

I asked her what she had felt.

She thought about it for a long time. "They were still there," she said. "Not their bodies. Them. Standing around me. And I think — I think Athena was there too. Pallas Athena. My namesake." She looked at me. "Socrates, this is the answer. Isn't it? A people whose laws are strong enough that they will die for them — that's what we lost. That's what I have to bring back."

I did not answer her at once. I was thinking about Sparta — about the helots who worked the Spartan fields under the lash, about the children Spartan law exposed on hillsides for being too small, about the secret killings the young men were sent out to perform as part of their training. I was thinking that obedience is only as good as the law obeyed, and the law only as good as the wisdom that made it. I was thinking, too, of Leonidas, who had stood at his post like a man in love with something larger than his own life, and whom I could not bring myself to dishonor by speaking against him at that moment.

So I said only, "Play it for me sometime, Pallas. I would like to hear it."

She nodded. We slept that night near the springs, and the smell of sulfur was in our hair when we woke, and the epitaph the poet Simonides would carve on this place was not yet carved, but I already knew the words.

Go, stranger, and tell the Lacedaemonians that we lie here, obedient to their laws.
