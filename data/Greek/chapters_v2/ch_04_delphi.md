The Oracle

I did not go to Delphi with the children. A man as old as I will find few answers in the vapours of a shape shifting priestess of Apollo — god of the sun, of prophecy, of music, and of medicine. What follows I pieced together later from the children's various accounts, each of them focusing on different aspects of their trip and offering details that the others left out.

They sailed west and north along the gulf until they came to Kirrha and climbed up. Anyone who has been to Delphi knows the feeling of that climb. The mountain rises so steeply that the sky seems to lean away from you. The terraces are cut into the rock as though some patient god had taken a chisel to the world and made room for a temple. The air is thin and cold even in summer. Eagles ride the wind above the cliffs of the Phaedriades, and far below, the olive groves of the plain run all the way to the sea.

The children walked up the Sacred Way. Dolios was reading every dedication aloud as though he had built them himself or wanted to meet the people who had. Here was the Athenian treasury, raised after Marathon, when our grandfathers turned back the Persian king Darius and his commanders at the plain north of Athens. Here was a great bronze dedicated after Salamis, when the wooden walls the oracle had promised turned out to be ships, and Themistocles understood the riddle in time to save Greece from Xerxes. Here were tripods and shields and a thousand small bronze gods, each one a quiet boast: we won, and we want the god to remember.

At the temple itself the four of them stopped. Above the entrance, cut into the stone in letters older than my grandfather, were two words. Phoebe read them aloud.

Γνῶθι σεαυτόν.

Know thyself.

She read them again, more slowly. She told me later that she had expected the temple of the god who knows everything to say something else. Knowest thou the god, perhaps. Or, listen here to find the answer. Instead, the inscription handed the question back to whomever stood at the door.

Inside, at the heart of the sanctuary, sat the omphalos — a stone shaped like a great egg, carved with a net of woolen bands. The Greeks call it the navel of the world. The story is that Zeus — king of the gods, whose sign is the eagle and the lightning bolt — released two eagles from the ends of the earth, and where they met, that was the center, and the center was here. The children stared at it. Klēta, who likes to know how things are made, walked all the way around it twice. Pallas wanted to know whether the priests believed it was truly the center, or whether they only said so. Dolios was already at work on a priest.

Dolios had decided that if these were the men who guarded the god's mouth, he would befriend them. He asked what their robes were made of. He admired the bronzes. He told one priest that his beard was the most distinguished beard he had seen in any city. The priest, who had probably heard worse flattery from kings, allowed himself a smile and let the children stand closer to the inner sanctuary than visitors usually stand. This is how Dolios moves through the world. I do not entirely approve. But I notice that doors open for him.

Phoebe stood before the omphalos for a long time without speaking. The others moved around her — Klēta examining the carved netting, Pallas questioning a priest about the stone's origins, Dolios having already made a friend of someone. Phoebe stood still. She had learned, in the storm, that the right response to a voice was to listen. She was listening now. She could not have said what she heard. Only that something was present, and that it was paying attention.

So they went in.

They told me the priestess sat over a chasm in the floor, breathing the vapors that rose from below, and that her voice, when it came, did not sound like a woman's voice. They told me she spoke in verse. They told me her words were these — and I will give them as the children gave them to me, because oracles are not the kind of thing one paraphrases:

Go where the few faced fire but did not burn.
Go where the sorrows of kings and peasants churn.
Go where the greatest stories outstrip the author's hand.
Go where the widest sea finally meets the land.

They came out into the sunlight and stood for a long time without speaking. Each child turned the words over and over again in their minds. This is the trouble with oracles. They're never as clear as one would like. Croesus of Lydia, before the Persian wars, asked the god whether he should attack Cyrus, and the god replied that if he did, he would destroy a great empire. Croesus, delighted, marched east and destroyed a great empire — his own. The Athenians, when Xerxes came, were told to trust their wooden walls. Half the city thought that meant the old palisade on the Acropolis, and the other half, with Themistocles leading them, thought it meant the fleet. Themistocles was right. The palisade burned. The oracle had said both things and neither, and the Athenians who chose correctly were the ones who already knew what to do.

And then of course there was my oracle. A wisdom grounded in ignorance.

But the children did not know any of this yet. They were standing on the slope of Parnassus with the light still in their eyes, their shadows sharp against the cliff wall. Each was still reaching for the answer that would make sense of the question they were carrying within.

They climbed back down to the harbor as the sun was going behind the mountains. The children told me later that they did not sleep that night. They talked and talked with one another on the deck of their strange boat and watched the stars move across the sky.

It was Phoebe who finally said what the oracle reminded her of. She had been quiet for a long time, lying on her back with her knees up, looking at nothing in particular.

She said she had read a story in their book earlier that day, while the others were occupied with priests and bronzes. The story of Perseus and the Gorgon.

Dolios said he knew it. Pallas said she knew it too. Klēta said she did not, and would Phoebe please tell it.

So Phoebe told it.

Perseus was the son of Zeus, which was a useful thing to be and also a dangerous one. His grandfather had locked his mother away after an oracle told him that his grandson would kill him, and Zeus had come to her anyway. When Perseus was born, his grandfather put mother and child in a chest and threw the chest into the sea. They washed up on an island. Perseus grew up there, the kind of young man who could be talked into anything, which is how he ended up promising to bring a king the head of Medusa.

Medusa was a Gorgon — one of three sisters who lived at the edge of the world, near the land of the dead. Anyone who looked directly at her face was turned to stone. There was no shortage of heroes who had tried. They were all still out there, in the dark, staring at nothing.

The gods equipped Perseus for what no man could survive alone. Hermes gave him a blade. Athena gave him a shield — polished to a mirror on the inside. The trick, Athena explained, was never to look at Medusa directly. Use the shield. Find her reflection in the bronze. Cut only what you see there.

Perseus found her sleeping. He held the shield and turned his face away and watched the reflection of a monster staring up at him from the polished bronze. He cut off her head without ever meeting her eyes.

From the wound, as the blood hit the sea foam, something extraordinary was born: Pegasus — the winged horse of the Muses, the carrier of poets and musicians, the horse who would kick the spring of inspiration from the rock of Mount Helicon with one blow of his hoof. Beauty rising from the thing that would have killed you if you looked at it straight on.

Perseus gave the head to Athena. She put it on her aegis, the terrible shield she wears into battle. The face that destroys is now the face of Wisdom herself, held outward at the world.

Phoebe stopped there. The others waited.

She said she thought that was what the oracle was doing.

Dolios asked what she meant.

She said: you cannot look at certain things directly. Truth that is too large — the truth about what you are, the truth about what a city is, the truth about what is good and what is just — if you approach it head-on it turns you to stone. You become one of the heroes petrified in the dark near the land of the dead, certain they had found the answer. The oracle gives you a mirror instead. A reflection. A glancing approach.

Klēta said that seemed like a good way for a god to avoid being held responsible.

Pallas said that Athena sounded fierce.

There was a long silence on the water. Above them the stars moved slowly in their fixed courses, each one certain of its position in a way that nothing below them was.

*Know thyself*, Phoebe said finally, very quietly, almost to herself, as she drifted into sleep.
