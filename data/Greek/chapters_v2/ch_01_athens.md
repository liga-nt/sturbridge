The spring I am about to describe was one of the last mild ones, and I was spending it as I spent most mild days: out of doors, in an argument.

My companions that afternoon — among them the young Plato, who had recently taken to carrying a scroll everywhere and writing things down, a habit I had always discouraged and now found I could not stop — had been debating, with considerable energy and very little agreement, the nature of the ideal state. Plato thought the ideal Republic would have philosopher-kings. His companion Glaucon thought it required courage above all. I had been pointing out, as patiently as I could manage, that none of us had yet established what justice actually was, which seemed like a reasonable thing to know before designing a city meant to contain it. We had been at this for some time. The afternoon light was agreeable. A fig tree provided adequate shade. I was, by my own modest accounting, quite content.

It was in the middle of all this that four children appeared.

They did not appear from nowhere — I want to be clear about that, because people who heard the story later added that detail, as people always do when they feel the truth is insufficiently dramatic. They walked up from the direction of the harbor like any ordinary visitors to the city. What was unusual was not how they arrived but how they looked: like people who had been carrying something very heavy for a very long time and were only beginning to notice how tired their arms were.

The oldest girl caught sight of me first. She was perhaps thirteen, dark-eyed, the kind of child who watches before she speaks. "You are Socrates," she said. It was not a question.

This is what people call me, I told her, though I had never been entirely certain it was the most accurate description.

"We need your help," she said. "Where we come from, things have gotten really bad."

By that description, I told her, you could have come from virtually anywhere. War and strife had become normal fare in the cities around the Aegean and Ionian seas.

"Not where," she continued, "I should have said when. We come from the future."

I looked at the young Plato. He looked back at me over his scroll with an expression I had come to recognize: the face of a man who sees a mistake approaching from a considerable distance and has calculated that there is nothing to be done about it.

The youngest boy — quick-eyed – offered a kind smile meant no doubt to dispell the storm clouds of skepticism. He spoke slowly and respectfully, "We can prove we're from the future, Mr. Socrates. We have a book."

This line of argument demonstrated to me better than anything that these children were not from Athens. You see, everyone in Athens would know that I distrust the written word and have never written down so much as a single thought — not because writing is useless, but because it creates a dangerous semblance of knowledge. A written argument looks the same whether it is true or false. It cannot answer your questions. It sits there, patient and immovable, saying the same thing to the wise man and the fool alike. So when a charming boy waves a book at me as proof of anything, I am, if anything, more skeptical than before. 

And yet — I must confess that this book was unlike any I had seen in the libraries and stalls of Athens. It was bound and flat, with a stiff cover of a kind I had never encountered. The pages were smooth as the Aegean on a windless morning and the illustrations, if that is what they were, had a quality that made me suspect something beyond ordinary human hands. The girl called Klēta opened it to a page near the middle and handed it to me. There was an illustration of the Acropolis. I recognized it immediately — the Parthenon, the Propylaea, the statue of Athena whose spear tip sailors use to navigate into Piraeus. But surrounding the illustration was text in a language I did not know. When I asked what it said, Klēta read it to me: the Parthenon, it said, had been built between 447 and 432 BCE. I did not know what BCE meant. I turned several pages and found a section on the Persian Wars, a map of the Aegean finer than any I had seen in any book stall, and, somewhat further on, a brief account of my own trial. At that point the boy Dolios lunged forward and closed the book with considerable force. "That section," he said, "is not so interesting. But the apologetic look on his face told me a different story. 

It's quite all right, I told him. I am not one to lose my head over a book. At that he relaxed and his native mirth returned.

Despite my growing fondness for these children, I was beginning to frame my excuses when the youngest girl — Klēta, ten years old, with eyes as sharp as a carving knife — offered something more persuasive than any text. "Our boat is down at Piraeus," she said. "If you want to see it."

Unlike books, I have always considered boats about the most honest things on earth. If an argument fails, the arguer usually denies it. But if a boat fails, it sinks to the bottom of the sea, and no one is deceived about whether it was a worthy vessel. The book had given me pause. The boat, I suspected, might give me something else entirely.

So we walked down to the harbor.
