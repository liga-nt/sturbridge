The Great Library

The children came back to me after a journey I could not follow even in imagination.

I have spent most of my life within a morning's walk of the Agora. I have been to Sparta once, and to Potidaea in my military service, and to Delium, and to a few other places the city required me to go. I have never been to Egypt. In my understanding it was a very old country that had been making things — calendars, monuments, mathematical observations — for longer than the Greeks had been building anything at all, and I had always felt a natural respect for this, mixed with the practical certainty that I would never see it.

The children went. When they described it to me afterward, each of them described a different city.

---

The boat left Athens in the year the plague still held the city, after the plays, after the long walk home. They had talked late into the night — I was asleep by the time the talking stopped. In the morning they were gone, and when they came back through the door three days later with the particular look of people who have been in the presence of too much information, I was once again where they had left me, with a book and an oil lamp.

Later I would learn that they had gone to the harbor, and that the harbor was wrong by three hours past midnight — the smells wrong, the constellations wrong, the air carrying the warmth of a different latitude entirely. The coast that came up on the right side was low and flat in a way no Greek coast is low and flat: an uninterrupted line of pale sand meeting a pale sky, with reeds and pelicans, and in the distance the wide brown smell of a river releasing itself into the sea.

The Nile, Klēta said. We're near its mouth.

They had come south through the Aegean and passed the island of Rhodes in the night — the whole long run of its eastern coast visible to their right, and then behind them, before any of them had quite registered how far they had come.

They had already passed what they were looking for before they recognized it. When they turned back, it was because Phoebe noticed the light — not a lighthouse light in the ordinary sense, but a fire on a tower so high it was visible before any other feature of the shore. They came around the headland and saw Alexandria.

---

There was nothing in Athens that prepared you for a harbor on that scale — nothing in the Aegean, which was large but broken into islands and made any individual stretch of water feel bounded. This was the open sea arriving at the open shore, the long flat Mediterranean delivering itself against the long flat coast of Egypt, and in between the two stood a city that had been built from nothing within living memory of its founders. Everything about it was intentional. The streets were straight. The harbor was recessed and protected by the island of Pharos, and on the island the lighthouse worked without ceasing, a bronze mirror catching fire and sending it fifty miles out to sea.

When Dolios saw it, he stopped walking.

He looked at it for a while. Then he said: who built that?

A man named Sostratos of Cnidus, Klēta said.

Is he famous?

He should be, Klēta said.

---

The head of the Library was a small, wide-foreheaded man who laughed easily and walked faster than anyone expected. He had been awake since before sunrise for what appeared to be the past decade. His name was Eratosthenes. He was not surprised by the arrival of four foreign children who had appeared at the harbor without any record of arriving by ship — the Library attracted all sorts, and he had learned to reserve his surprise for things that were actually surprising.

He told them that his colleagues called him Beta. Second-best geographer. Second-best mathematician. Second-best poet. A man who was second-best at ten things had something to recommend him over a man who was first at one: he knew what it felt like to look at everything, and everything had looked back.

He's a polymath, Kleta said. That means he is learned in more things than he should be.

He showed them the hall, the catalog system, the shelving logic. He mentioned in passing that the Library had a policy of copying everything that came in by ship — not always with the owners' consent, but promptly. Klēta received all of this with the expression of someone calculating how much time she has and whether it is enough.

It is not enough, Eratosthenes told her, but most people find it is still worth starting.

He smiled and left them to it.

---

Klēta went directly to the history shelves.

She knew some of the events from the book she'd brought on their trip, but she had come to realize that history means something different from the eyes of someone living it. She started at the beginning.

She read Thucydides account of the plague — he described symptoms in clinical sequence: the throat, the chest, the hands, the mind. Bodies in the temples because people had nowhere else to go. The familiar restraints — reputation, law, the fear of punishment — dissolving in the arithmetic of survival. Men who had never stolen from a neighbor taking what was near to hand, because they expected to be dead by morning. The man who had written these sentences was thirty years old and sick and watching his city die around him and had still believed that precision was the correct response.

She read through the Peace of Nicias, established in 421 BCE and lasting seven years in which both sides were exhausted and neither admitted it. Then the vote on Sicily — she could see the disaster coming from six books away, and so could he, writing it backward from knowledge, and still he recorded the debate without comment: Nicias arguing against, Alcibiades for, the assembly deciding with the enthusiasm of people who have not yet understood what they are deciding. The expedition. The siege of Syracuse in 415 BCE when Athens sent two hundred ships and forty thousand men across the Ionian Sea to take Sicily and came back with almost nothing — the survivors dying in open stone quarries south of Syracuse, in the heat, with no shelter and insufficient food for seventy days. 

By the time she had gotten through Book VIII, she was in Athens, 411 BCE: the city attempting to overthrow its own democracy, the treasury stripped, the fleet badly damaged, the best general exiled by the very assembly he had led. Theramenes is making a speech about the composition of the governing council, and Thucydides is recording the particulars of his argument, and then —

The scroll ended.

Not with a conclusion. The last word was mid-sentence. The next word was nowhere.

She looked at the shelf and then back at the scroll.

The catalog said Thucydides had lived until about 400 BCE — another decade after the war ended, another decade after Athens was finally defeated and the Long Walls came down. He had returned from exile in 404 BCE when the Thirty recalled him, an old man coming home to a city he had watched destroy itself from a distance. He had those years to finish. But Book VIII was drier than the others — no speeches in the grand style, just events in sequence, the writing of a first draft. He had been revising. He had not finished.

She thought of him the night before in the north stoa, pointing at his tablet, telling her he did not know what came next. He did not know that he would be sent out as a general in 424 BCE and then exiled for arriving too late; that he would spend roughly 20 years in Thrace, only to be recalled in 404 when Athens had been defeated and the Spartans had torn down the long wall to the sounds of music, replacing democracy with a rule of Thirty Tyrants. He had believed that if you looked carefully enough at what men did and why, you could extract from the record something durable. He had looked carefully and extracted something. He had not lived to set it down in its final form.

Klēta paused before she picked up the next scroll. She knew she would never read another historian the way she read Thucydides with that vivid image of him gesturing at his work as Athenians died around him. But she also knew she would now sense the author behind any scroll in a new way. 

She unrolled the beginning of the next scroll on the shelf.

A man named Xenophon had picked up exactly where Thucydides stopped — not explaining himself, not announcing a continuation. He wrote: After this, not many days later... and continued the narrative. Klēta marvelled at this choice, to continue the work of a man he no doubt admired a great deal. She read through to the battle of Aegospotami in 405 BCE, where the Spartan navy destroyed the Athenian fleet and ended the war that had swallowed a generation.

When she finished the Xenophon scroll she picked up Theopompus. His work overlapped that of Xenophon, writing about some of the same events but in a grander more extravagant, moralizing style that Klēta didn't particularly care for.

She read in both authors about Philip II of Macedon, a king from the north who had looked at the condition of Athens and the other Greek cities — tired, poor, arguing with each other about governance in the way that exhausted people argue about small things — and had decided this was an opportunity. Theopompus disapproved of Philip personally and professionally, which meant his account was sometimes more interested in Philip's vices than his strategy, but even disapproval could not entirely obscure the efficiency of the man: he was simply better at organizing an army than anyone since Themistocles had been better at organizing a navy, and it was the same quality — not inspiration, but the accumulation of small improvements, applied systematically.

At Chaeronea, in what Klēta surmised was 338 BCE, Philip's cavalry broke the Sacred Band of Thebes on the right flank and defeated both Thebes and Athens. The commander of the cavalry on that flank was eighteen years old. His name was Alexander. He inherited his father's logistics and something else besides — a fury for conquest, for whatever country was farthest away, for the place where the maps ran out. He crossed into Asia and broke Darius III at the Battle of Issus in 333 BCE, near the sea. He went further and broke him again at Gaugamela in 331 BCE, in the open plain. He kept going until even the geography ran out and finally died in 323 BCE. The age that followed — the Hellenistic Age — was the Library's own era, built on everything he had scattered across the world.

Klēta stood at the shelf with the scrolls around her.

The shelf continued.

There were accounts of Alexander's campaigns written by men who had marched in them. There were histories of Egypt before Alexander. There was a mathematician who had written about the shape of the earth. There were accounts of India. There were accounts of places west of Carthage that she had never heard of.

She stood for a long time, looking at the shelf, and imaging the weave of all these voices, lives, and events.

---

While Klēta contemplated the fabric of history, Pallas had embarked upon her own literary pursuits. She had asked Eratosthenes for the philosophical section and been directed with a brisk wave down a long corridor and to the left. She found the treatise on poetry after some searching — it was well-worn, more copies made than she had expected.

She read slowly.

Aristotle had not liked Euripides much, as far as she could tell. He considered him a less perfect craftsman than Sophocles. But he also called him the most tragic of the poets. She read this sentence twice and went forward.

The argument was about what tragedy did. Not what it meant — Aristotle was impatient with the idea that tragedy was primarily about meaning. Tragedy was an imitation of an action: complete, of a certain magnitude, structured to produce a specific effect. The effect was the catharsis of pity and fear through their arousal and discharge. You came to the theater carrying both feelings already inside you, accumulated from living your life in the ordinary way, and the play drew them out — made them specific, gave them a shape — and then spent them. You left lighter than you arrived. Not because nothing bad had happened, but because you had experienced the bad thing safely, at a remove, and your pity and fear had been used up against something that was not you.

She sat with this.

She did not write anything down. She sat with the scroll and the garden outside the window, where a palm tree moved slightly in the afternoon air, and she let the afternoon go by.

---

Dolios had lasted twenty-three minutes in the Library before he needed to leave.

He walked until the grid of the Greek quarter gave way to something else — wider doorways, different proportions, the smell of different oils in the lamps and different spices in the food stalls. The market near the eastern harbor had six languages competing at once and appeared to understand itself anyway. He bought a piece of flatbread from a man who did not speak any Greek, thanked him in Greek, and received a look of polite incomprehension followed by a smile. He ate the bread on a pier and watched the ships.

An older man was mending a net nearby and seemed willing to have company. Dolios helped, badly, and was shown how to do it correctly. They worked in silence for a while.

He had been a soldier. Not a famous soldier — he had marched in Alexander's eastern campaigns, been at Gaugamela as a spear-carrier in the third rank, seen the cavalry break the Persian line from too far back to see anything except the direction the running was going. He had marched further east afterward, into places the Greeks had no names for, on roads made of dust of a particular color he had not seen before or since. He had come back, not home — he did not particularly have a home anymore — but to Alexandria, because it was the nearest thing to nowhere that was still somewhere.

Did Alexander lead from the front? Dolios asked.

Always, the man said. Every engagement. He took an arrow in the shoulder at one of the eastern sieges and was directing the assault from the wall with one arm while the surgeons argued about the angle. Men followed him because he went where he was sending them. That was the whole of it.

Was he a great man?

The man looked at his net for a moment. Then he said: I don't know what that means. He was the best at what he did that I have seen, and I have seen most of what was available to see. He died at thirty-two never finishing what he started and without any way to preserve it.

He tied off a strand.

The city exists, he said. You can live in the city and not think about how it was made, or you can think about how it was made and live somewhere else. He shrugged. I'm too old to care which is correct. But I'm here.

Dolios sat listening to the water against the piers and the six languages competing in the background. On his way back toward the boat he passed a flat stone near the water's edge with a small heap of grain scattered on it — an offering, or old habit. Demeter, goddess of the harvest and of grain, he thought, even here.

---

Phoebe had asked Eratosthenes about the Egyptian temples before the others had gone to their sections. He had pointed south, across the narrow strip of water separating the Greek quarter from the older shore, and described the pylons — the thick trapezoidal gates — by which she would know it.

She walked until the harbor sounds were behind her and the proportions of the buildings changed.

The temple was old in a way that the Library was not. The Library was twenty years old. The Parthenon, which had seemed to her the oldest building she had seen, was less than seventy years old when she had stood beneath it. This temple was older than Athens. It had been here when the Greeks were just beginning to write down their myths, and before that, and before that.

Inside, the walls were covered with painted reliefs — figures in profile, each one doing something specific and purposeful. Near the inner hall there was a long sequence she looked at for a time: a woman moving through a dark country, bent forward, hands open, searching. The posture was not grief, exactly. It was more purposeful than grief. Grief stands still. This figure was walking.

A priest came and stood beside her without speaking.

She is looking for something, Phoebe said.

Everything, the priest said. The goddess is Isis and she is searching for the dismembered parts of her husband's body.

Is this the end of the story?

No. She could not find all the parts, but she never stopped searching, and for the brief time she had him well enough put together, they concieved their son Horus who became a king of Egypt. He paused. This is why people come here. Not to be told that loss is temporary but that gathering up what remains is what makes life after loss possible.

Phoebe stood in the hall for some time. When she came back out into the afternoon, the light over the harbor was turning orange.

---

They found each other near the pier where the boat was moored. Klēta was carrying three scrolls she had arranged to have copied — she had apparently negotiated this with Eratosthenes using the argument that she came from a place without a Library and the information would not be wasted. Dolios smelled like salt water and looked more settled than he had in several days. Pallas had the look of someone who has been sitting still for a long time and is not yet ready to talk about why.

The harbor was doing its evening business. Ships came in with the last of the wind. The Pharos worked overhead.

None of them said much. The city moved around them — a language they didn't recognize, the smell of different wood burning, the Pharos light beginning its work across the water. After a while Phoebe looked back the way they had come, toward the open sea, and then toward the boat.

We should go back to Athens, she said.

Nobody disagreed.

---

I should tell you what I made of all this when the children came back through the door.

They each described something different. Klēta described the shelves. Dolios described the man mending the net and the delicious flatbread. Pallas described tragedy in quite provocative way, one that I had never heard before but nevertheless sounded vaguely familiar. Phoebe described the posture of the figure on the temple wall — the forward lean, the open hands.

When Klēta told me about the end of Thucydides, I asked what she had felt.

She said: I thought there would be a conclusion. I thought the scroll itself might feel like a conclusion — that the last sentence might have a different weight. She shook her head. It's just a sentence.

The continuation was what surprised her, she said. Not the ending but the continuation. Xenophon taking up the pen the same day, or the next, not explaining himself, just writing what came next. Then the next person. Then the next. I smiled, for I knew Xenophon well and I wasn't at all surpised by her account.

I could have kept reading for my entire life, she said.

Dolios chimed in, there's a story about this right, someone who spends their entire life reading and then realizes that they didn't do any living and the gods punish them by turning them into a book.

Everyone laughed, Klēta most of all. One could do worse, Dolios, than turning into a book, she said. 

It was then that I felt compelled to tell the children of the trial I would be facing the next morning, a trial at which I would be forced to give a speech in defense of my virtue. It's 399 Klēta said! Your trial. The kids' faces dropped.

You needn't be worried, I said. If I've lived my life well, according to my conscience, what can I fear from some false accusations?

Uh, death? Dolios remarked.

Well, at my age, young man, death seems like it might be just a nice place to sleep, a wide spreading olive tree under which I might recline. In any case, you needn't attend the trial, it is likely to be a spectacle though I'll do my best to prevent it.

No, Phoebe said, I think this is where the Oracle is leading us at last.

Is that so? I said.

Yes, Phoebe said. Where the widest sea finally meets the land.
