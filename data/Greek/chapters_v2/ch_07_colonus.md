Oedipus at Colonus

The seating swept down the hillside in a long curve — tier after tier of cut stone, already filling when we arrived. At the bottom, the orchestra: a circle of packed earth with the altar of Dionysus at its center — the god of wine and of theater, his altar garlanded with ivy and grape vines for the festival. Beyond it, the painted stage building. And beyond that, open air, the city below, and the silver line of the sea.

Pallas stopped walking.

She stood at the top of the entrance ramp for a moment without saying anything, taking in the spectacle of the crowd the theater.

How many people, she said.

Fifteen thousand, I said. More on a good year.

We found places about a third of the way up. Dolios was immediately in conversation with the man beside him — and within minutes had secured us better seats that we should have gotten. Klēta had her book out. Phoebe sat very still with her hands in her lap and watched the altar below.

The chorus entered.

Fifteen men in identical masks and robes, moving together into the orchestra with a slow, measured step that was not quite walking and not quite dancing. When they began to speak — it shifted between speech and song, so that you were never quite sure which it was — the sound was unlike a single voice in the way that a river is unlike a cup of water. The masks turned in unison and the voices moved with them.

Oedipus entered from the side — blind, led by the hand by a young woman who was Antigone. He moved like a man who had learned to navigate by something other than sight: weight forward, one foot certain before the next. The mask was an old man's face, fixed and expressionless. From twenty rows back, Pallas watched it as though it were moving.

He didn't know where he was. He sat on a stone at the edge of a sacred grove and a man from the chorus told him to get up — this was holy ground, no one sat there, he had to leave. Oedipus said he would not leave. He knew this place. He had been told about it.

The story came out in pieces, as it always does in these plays. Who he was. What he had done. The plague. The exile. His sons who did not come for him, and his daughters who did. The oracle that had sent him wandering, and the other oracle that told him where it would end: a city that received him would receive his protection when he was gone. This city. This grove.

Creon arrived from Thebes. He had the pleasant manner of a man who does not expect to be refused. He explained, in careful terms, that Oedipus was coming back to Thebes. He had Oedipus's daughters seized while he was talking. He moved to take Oedipus himself.

Then Theseus came.

I have seen this play performed several times since, and this is always the moment the crowd goes quiet. Not the seizure of the daughters — that produces noise, outrage — but this: Theseus walking in and simply receiving Oedipus. He already knew the full story. He had every reason an Athenian king could need to send this cursed, ruined man somewhere else. He offered sanctuary anyway, without ceremony, as a matter of course.

I glanced at Pallas.

She was watching the audience.

Not the stage — the crowd. Fifteen thousand Athenians in a curve around the orchestra, watching a story about their city choosing to do the decent thing. She moved her eyes slowly from one section to another, the way someone does when they are counting, or measuring, or trying to understand how something holds together.

She noticed me looking. She turned back to the stage.

The play moved toward its end. Oedipus knew when his time had come — the thunder said so, three times, each one closer than the last. He asked Theseus to come with him alone, to witness and keep secret the place of his death, so that the power of it would stay with Athens. He walked off toward the grove.

He did not come back.

Antigone and Ismene stood in the orchestra and wept. The chorus tried to console them with the only thing the chorus ever has to offer, which is that the dead are past their suffering.

The play ended. People were slow to get up.

The kids didn't move at all. So much of what they were seeing was new to them and when I looked at their faces, I could see that they were tired. I hoped that Athens could take on their weariness as it had for Oedipus. Before I could make any suggestions, Phoebe lept up and said firmly.

We need to go visit the Prytaneion.

I wasn't sure how she knew of the Prytaneion or what she hoped to find there, but we filed out of the theater and followed and followed her down through the city toward the Agora until we reached the Prytaneion, the building which kept the city's official records, and its common hearth — Hestia's fire, burning without interruption as long as anyone could remember, tended by the city's magistrates as a civic duty. The Prytaneion was also Athens' table of hospitality, offering meals to olympic victors, foreign dignataries, citizens who had performed exceptional service, the children of men killed by Athenian tyrants, and a rotating group of city magistrates. When we arrived, Phoebe went immediately to the fires and to a priestess who tended them. She went up close and whispered something to the priestess and immediately followed her through a door I had not previously seen. We waited what seemed like 10 or 20 minues when Dolios finally spoke up.

I am really hungry Socrates. He said that so much had happened he couldn't remember when he had last eaten. Just as he spoke Phoebe came out and beckoned us to come. We followed her through that same door and through two more until we were in room that expressed simplicity and abundance at the same time. Couches were arranged in a circle with platters filled with meats and olives and bread and oil. The priestess was showing each child to a couch and place me in a spot near Phoebe.

How did you manage this, I asked her with a smile.

I just told her we were travelers from the future and sought refuge with Hestia. It took me a while to convince her that people who travel from the future, no matter how ordinary, tend to be dignataries. But once I convinced her, she told me the goddess had long ago told her she'd receive important guests. 

Remarkable, I said. But when I looked at the priestess' face, she looked quite troubled. She doesn't look well, I told Phoebe. Did the goddess tell her something else?

Yes, Phoebe said. The goddess told her that once the guests arrived, she wouldn't see another full cycle of the sun.

As the evening wore on, I saw the priestess release the heaviness of this news and take joy in speaking with each of the children as they ate their fill. After the meal, the priestess came around the room and dimmed the oil lamps. The rest of the children had already fallen asleep on the couches, but Phoebe and priestess were still whispering quietly with one another when morpheus overcame me.
