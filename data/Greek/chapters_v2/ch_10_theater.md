What the Poets Knew

The city they walked into was not the city of Pallas's dream.

The streets were quieter than they should have been. Houses sat with their shutters closed against the midmorning. At the corner of a narrow alley two men were carrying something wrapped in cloth, not quickly, and the people around them moved out of the way without looking. The Kerameikos, where the potters worked and the great funeral processions gathered, had acquired a second function in the past two years that no one discussed directly. The plague had been moving through Athens since 430. It had taken roughly one in three.

They walked toward the Acropolis. Pallas looked up at the Parthenon from the road below.

In her dream it had been white the way marble is white when it has just been cut — the color of something that has never been looked at long enough to be diminished. What she saw now was stone. Still young stone, less than fifteen years weathered, but beginning the slow work of becoming a ruin. The columns were the color of cream going to yellow. She looked for a long time without saying what she was thinking.

Then Pallas stopped walking.

The crowd ahead of them had sorted itself out — not toward the Agora, not up toward the Acropolis. Toward the theater.

They're still going, Pallas said.

The city has been burying its people for two years, and they were still going to the theater.

---

I was forty-two years old the year Euripides' Hippolytus was performed for the first time and the Medea was reprised along side it. I had made a point of attending every performance of Euripides's plays at the Dionysia — he had been writing since I was a young man, and his work made me more uncomfortable than any of the others, a sign perhaps that there was something true in it. When the children described that year to me from their perspective — the crowd, the smell of the torches, the peculiar hush before the chorus enters — it matched my own memory closely enough that I had no doubt they had been there.

---

Medea was not new that year. Euripides had staged it three years before, in the first year of the war, and it had placed third. This tells you something about Athens, and something about Euripides, who placed third more often than not and kept writing anyway.

The story begins before the play begins. Jason had sailed to Colchis on the Black Sea to take the Golden Fleece — a commission that should have been impossible, and would have been, except that Hera — queen of the gods, whose ancient domain is marriage — was his protector through all of it. She had reasons of her own: Pelias had insulted her once, and she had not forgotten. She smoothed the way for Jason and his Argonauts across every sea and obstacle. Medea was a woman of considerable power in her own right, the daughter of the king of Colchis, and she helped Jason in exchange for a promise. She left her home and her people and her brother. She followed him to Corinth.

In Corinth, Jason found a more convenient arrangement. The king's daughter. A Greek girl, which Medea was not — she was a foreigner, a barbarian, and useful only as long as he needed her. She stood in the orchestra and you watched her decide what to do.

She killed the princess and her father. Then she killed her own children. Not in a fit of passion: after considering it, step by step. She did it so that Jason would have nothing left. Then she escaped in a chariot sent by her grandfather Helios, and the play ended.

Dolios walked out before the end. He came back ten minutes later and sat down without explaining himself.

Pallas stayed for all of it. What she noticed, she told me later, was that Euripides showed each step of Medea's reasoning — not to excuse it, but to make it visible. Each decision followed from the one before. You could trace the whole thing backward to the original promise and how it had been broken.

What do you mean exactly, I asked her.

She said: Clytemnestra killed for Iphigeneia. That's terrible, but you can follow the logic — vengeance for a daughter, a wrong that could be made into law. The Oresteia made a court out of it. Medea has no court. What she has is: he took everything and gave nothing back. And the answer to that, in her hands, was total destruction. No one comes to transform it. No Athena appears. It ends the way it ends, and the play stops.

---

In the evening they watched Hippolytus.

The goddess Aphrodite — goddess of love and beauty — opens the play by walking out and stating her case. Hippolytus has devoted himself entirely to Artemis and refuses to honor Aphrodite at all. For this she will destroy him. She says this plainly, at the beginning, before any of the human action. It is her right. That is the whole premise.

What follows is Phaedra — Theseus's wife, Hippolytus's stepmother, cursed by Aphrodite to love him. Phaedra knows what the feeling is, where it comes from, and would rather die than speak of it. Her nurse disagrees. There is a long scene in which the nurse persuades her. A letter gets written. Hippolytus reads it and refuses her with horror. Phaedra, certain he will tell, kills herself and leaves the letter accusing him of attacking her instead.

Theseus reads the letter. He banishes his son and calls down the curse he holds from Poseidon. A monster rises from the sea near the road. The horses bolt. Hippolytus dies of his injuries on the shore.

Artemis arrives. She is too late to save anyone, but she can at least make sure Theseus knows what he did. She tells him everything. He knows. Hippolytus is dead anyway.

Dolios said quietly: so Aphrodite and Artemis are fighting and the humans are just caught between them.

Yes, Phoebe said.

He thought about this for a moment.

And Hippolytus is destroyed because he honored one of them and not the other. Not because he did anything wrong.

Yes, Phoebe said.

I'm getting pretty tired of these gods and goddesses, Kleta said. Not to mention at this human theater.

---

In the crowd moving out of the theater, a man stopped next to Pallas. Perhaps sixty, with the look of someone who had seen the original Medea and every Euripides since. He said, without preamble: even Atalanta couldn't outrun Aphrodite.

Can you explain, Pallas asked. 

The old man stopped, seemingly happy to tell these strange children a story from his own childhood.

Atalanta was a huntress, he said. Devoted to Artemis from childhood — the hunt, the open hills, nothing else. Artemis, goddess of the moon and of the hunt, who carries a silver bow and keeps the company of deer and hunting dogs, was the only deity Atalanta honored. Not unlike Hippolytus. She vowed she would never marry, and she backed the vow with conditions: any man who wanted to marry her had to race her, and if he lost, he died. She was very fast. Every suitor lost.

Hippomenes wanted to marry her but knew he couldn't beat her in a race. He went to Aphrodite, who gave him three golden apples. During the race he dropped them one by one. Atalanta stopped each time to pick one up, and he won. They married.

Then they forgot to honor Aphrodite. She turned them both into lions.

There we go, Dolios said. I almost thought it was going to be a happy ending.

So devotion to Artemis was never protection, Kleta said. Not even from the beginning.

No, the old man said. Take it from me. Aphrodite always wins. He smiled and seemed to retreat into his own memories as he parted from them.

The children walked without speaking for a while. Then Pallas began: in the Oresteia, after all the killing — after Agamemnon, after Clytemnestra, after everything — Athena made a court. Blood feud was replaced by law. And at Colonus, Oedipus arrived broken and finished, but Theseus received him. Gave him dignity. And Athens became a place of refuge for the downtrodden. In each story, Athens becomes stronger through suffering and tragedy. But in Euripides, Medea's children are dead. Hippolytus is dead. Nobody makes a court. Nobody receives anyone. In Euripides tragedy is like a plague. It takes and it takes without giving anything in return.

But what this also means, Pallas said, is that art is not our answer. The greatest human achievements fall before the petty whims and jealousies of the gods.

---

Pallas told me that Phoebe and Dolios had been listening to her attentively but that when she stopped talking, she noticed Kleta had gone.

Looking all around, they noticed her sitting in the shade of the north stoa by a man with a wax tablet. It was our great historian Thucydides, and he was talking to Klēta with wide gestures and pointing repeatedly at the tablet on his lap. He was perhaps thirty-two, slightly built, with a body that looked older than his years — he had caught the plague himself, two years before, and survived it. This is the only reason we have his account of what it does to a person from the inside.

Klēta later told me that he showed his account of Pericles' Funeral Oration — the speech he had given at the end of the first year of the war, for those who died in it. Every citizen equal before the law. We are open to the world, to every form of talent. We love beauty without extravagance, wisdom without softness. We are the school of all Greece. The sentences were carefully made, load-bearing, each one carrying its share. When asked how he remembered it, Thucydides acted like he didn't understand the question.

He also showed her what he was writing that very day, about the plague — part of what he intended to be a complete History of the Peloponnesian War, 431 to 404 BCE. That section was not like the oration. Bodies in the temples. Men who had never stolen from a neighbor taking what was near to hand, because they expected to be dead by morning. The familiar restraints — reputation, law, the fear of punishment — had dissolved in the arithmetic of survival. No one kept oaths to gods who had not kept the plague from their houses.

She said she'd asked him: what comes next? In the war.

He looked at her directly. I don't know. I'm writing it as it happens. Sparta won't break and Athens won't yield and the whole thing will continue until one of them is brought to its knees.

She nodded, thanked him, and went to find the others.

When the kids re-entered the Prytaneion that evening, they found me just where they'd left me, 27 years in the future with a book and an oil lamp. I was happy to see them.

That evening, Klēta ate quickly and then set down her food.

I understand the third line of the oracle she said.

"Go where the greatest stories outstrip the author's hand."

The others looked at her expectantly.

Alexandria, she said.

What's in Alexandria, Dolios asked, another battle? I hope it's not another play.

A Library, Klēta said. Perhaps the most famous one in all of history. It contains virtually all the knowledge in the world. It'll allow us to travel the world and through time without moving anything but a few pages. Well, more like unrolling a few scrolls.

It's a good idea, Phoebe said. Pallas agreed. I didn't want to say anything myself – I had never heard of Alexandria, and I'd already told them how I felt about the written word. Thucydides had tried to hone in on the causes of things, but I'd always felt that the causes driving history were a bit different than the ones he named. Still, a sea voyage would do these children good and I thought that Klēta was probably right about the direction the oracle was now guiding them.

We spent the next few hours talking and laughing. Dolios insisted on giving us some comic relief, after the plauge, after Euripides. And none of us felt the worse for his efforts.
