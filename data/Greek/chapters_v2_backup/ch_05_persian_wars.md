Fire from the East

They told me that it was Dolios who woke first, while the others were still asleep on the deck.

He had dreamed of Hermes.

I have no settled opinion on whether the god actually appeared or whether it was the kind of dream that merely carries the weight of a divine visit. Hermes is, among other things, the god of the border between sleep and waking — the guide of souls through passages not quite of this world. The distinction between appearing and seeming to appear may not have been meaningful to him. What I can say is what Dolios described, and he described it with the particular precision of a child who has seen something he cannot explain and is trying very hard to sound like someone who is used to explaining things.

He appeared small at first, Dolios said, though the size kept shifting. His sandals had wings at the ankle — small, feathered wings, moving even when he stood still, which unsettled Dolios until he realized this was simply how Hermes stands. He carried a staff: a winged staff with two serpents coiled around each other from base to tip, both of them alive, intertwined in a way that was not quite fighting and not quite friendship. He moved the way thought moves — there before you have quite decided to think him. "He is the messenger of the gods," Dolios told the others, once they were awake and gathered around him on the deck. "He carries instructions between the worlds. The guide of travelers and inventors and —" he paused, considering how to frame this next part — "people who are very good at talking their way through difficult situations."

Pallas asked Dolios whether he was describing Hermes or himself.

"What can I say," Dolios smiled, "he's a charming guy for a god." But the point, Dolios continued, was what Hermes said. That the first line of the oracle was not a riddle but a direction. Go where the few faced fire but did not burn. He told Dolios that meant the narrow pass between the cliffs and the sea, where three hundred men held the world's largest army for three days. He said the ship would know the way. And he said that this time it would not be Poseidon driving them — it would be him. "I guide souls through passages," he said, in the dream. "What is time but a very long passage?"

Phoebe had been listening from the bow. This is right, she said. The first line belongs to Dolios and we must follow him.

So they went.

The children told me later that the shift was less violent than the storm. The stars in the sky began to move — not quickly at first, but with the slow deliberate certainty of a wheel beginning to turn. The season changed around them, the spring air becoming the heavy heat of summer. They held on to the sides of the boat and did not speak. By the time the stars had settled back into stillness, the coastline to their left was the same coastline — the claw of Attica reaching into the Aegean — but the villages were different. They were at Marathon.

The plain came to the shore here, flat and wide, running between the mountains and the sea. They landed at first light. The damp sand was smooth and the morning was quiet. No armies. No ships. Only the plain, the mountains, and a long low mound of earth at the edge of the marsh that Klēta pointed to, because it was the kind of earthwork that looked deliberate.

A soldier was sitting at the base of the mound, tending his armor with a cloth. Not a young man — old enough to have been here before. When the children asked what the mound was, he set down his armor.

Ten years ago, he said. 490 BCE. This is where we buried ours.

The Persian king Darius had sent his generals across the sea — a vast fleet, tens of thousands of men, landing here on this very shore. The Athenians sent to Sparta for help. The Spartans could not come; they were observing the festival of the Carneia, a sacred truce that not even war could interrupt. So Athens and Plataea faced the Persians alone. The Athenian generals argued about whether to fight or wait. Then Miltiades, who had lived among the Persians and understood them, said: our one advantage is speed. Persian archers are lethal at range. We must close the distance fast enough that the arrows do not find us. So they ran. Nine thousand men at a full charge across an open plain toward an army that had conquered half the world. The Persians, who had never seen a Greek army run at them, did not know what to make of it. Forty minutes later six thousand Persians were dead and the rest were in the water.

He was quiet for a moment. Then: but that was then. Darius is dead. His son Xerxes has come with ten times the army his father brought. They are already north of the Hellespont, already marching. He crossed the strait on a bridge of boats — two pontoon bridges lashed side by side, wide enough to march an army across. The Greeks said he whipped the Hellespont itself when a storm destroyed his first bridge. This is the kind of detail that sounds invented and turns out to be true. He tapped the mound with one hand. We stopped them here ten years ago. Today we will fight at Thermopylae, but I fear we may not have the gods on our side again.

He picked up his armor. He was going north.

Dolios stood on the plain for a long time without speaking. "It's all just so fragile." he finally said, "Ten years ago, the Athenians thought they had faced the greatest threat they could possibly imagine. They rushed onto the plain of Marathon filled with courage and emerged as heroes. Now they're setting out to face an even greater foe..."

Kleta put her hand on his shoulder. "Come on, we have a ways to go to reach Thermopylae in time."

The kids ran together down to the beach and pushed their boat out onto the water. Pallas set their tack and a favourable wind filled their sails. It's Hermes, Dolios thought, guiding us where we need to go.

They sailed north along the length of Euboea. At its tip, where the island narrows to a point, they found the Greek fleet — hundreds of triremes anchored in the channel, the Athenian ships among them, oars shipped, crews sitting on their benches waiting. This was Artemisium. While Leonidas held the land pass, the Greek fleet was here to hold the sea. The children did not stop. They sailed on into the Malian Gulf.

When they reached the gulf — it was 480 BCE, Klēta knew from her book, the same summer as the battle they had just left — the children could find no word for what they were seeing. Trireme after Persian trireme, stretching shore to shore, so many ships that the fleet resembled a forest stretching as far as the eye could see. Dolios counted until the counting became meaningless and stopped.

Then the marvelous sight was interrupted by a sharp, pungent smell. Phoebe said it was what she imagined the underworld smelled like. Klēta said it was the springs — she had read about them. The hot gates. Sulfur rising off the water.

The cliff face of the mountain dropped almost straight to the sea, and between the cliff and the water ran a corridor — barely wider, in places, than a cart. The pass of Thermopylae. It looked like something a god had cut into the rock as a test, or a joke. Three hundred Spartans were there, under their king Leonidas, and with them several thousand Greeks from other cities who had come in time. But many other Greeks were already retreating south along the coastal road when the children arrived — moving past the shoreline in a long, unhurried file, the way men move when they have been told to go but cannot make themselves hurry. Some looked back. Most did not.

Dolios climbed to the high ground above the road without waiting for the others, and they followed. The retreating men passed below — Thebans, Corinthians, men from a dozen cities he had never heard of, their shields muddy, their faces flat with something that was not quite shame and not quite relief. Dolios was trying to make out what they were saying to each other but could not. "Why are they leaving? Why are they retreating?" Dolios asked. 

Kleta came and stood beside him. "The Persians are too many for them. 300 Spartans are staying to fight because they take an oath to never retreat. Some Thespians have also stayed to fight with them. This small force will distract the Persians while the rest of the Greeks flee home to help the women, children, and elders evacuate. 

"But a small force like that could never survive the Persian army."

"No," Kleta said, "they couldn't. They won't. By now they are completely surrounded. One traitorous Greek showed them a path through the mountain and the Spartan forces has retreated to higher ground."

The children could not see the position of the Spartans, but the sound that struck their ears was undeniable. Bronze clashed against bronze, men shouted war cries. Worse sounds followed. When the sun set the clamour of battle began to trail off, leaving a heavy silence.

The kids struck camp on the beach and slept under blankets that Socrates has given them for the journey. Pallas could hear Dolios crying before exhaustion overtook him.

Dolios dreamed of Hermes again, but this time the god came differently — not bright and quick as he had appeared on the boat at Piraeus, but darker, lower, like a shape in smoke, the caduceus held at his side. He said nothing. He simply turned and walked and Dolios understood he was meant to follow.

The Persian camp was vast in a way the fleet had only suggested. The fires went on so far that their edges disappeared into dark. He followed Hermes through the fires until the god stopped beside one and was gone.

The soldier was an Immortal — one of the Persian elite, the ten thousand who had come around the mountain path in the night. He was sitting alone, not sleeping, staring at the fire. When Dolios appeared the man looked at him without surprise, the way people in dreams accept what is in front of them.

Dolios asked what had happened in the pass.

The man was quiet for a moment. Before the fighting began, I was boasting to one of our prisoners – a Spartan scout who had been caught unaware and was angry only that we would not allow him to die with is brothers. I told him our arrows would be so many they would block out the sun. Wonderful, he said. It is not often we have the benefit of fighting in the shade. The way he said it made me stop my boasting.

We expected the Spartans to hold their position, or to run. Instead they came forward into the open with a slow deliberate gait that we recognized at once. You can see when a soldier moves like that on the battlefield, you can see that they have decided to die and the only question is how many soldiers they would send down to meet Hades in the underworld — towering over them with his unforgiving bident, ruler of everything that does not return. When they fight with that type of spirit, they resemble gods more than men. As though Athena herself had breathed ambrosia upon their faces and heads.

Defeating them took a long time. Much longer than it should have. When their king Leonidas fell in the fighting, both sides fought over his body as if that one task would decide the outcome of the battle. You don't have to believe me but when I looked up from the fighting I saw towering above the plain Ares himself, sweat pouring down his body and eyes wide. He brandished his magnificent spear and above his head circled vultures who were waiting eargerly for their portion of the fight. We finally broke their ranks and every soldier to the very last one was killed. Not one of them ran. Never before have I seen so many soldiers die without a single man taking flight.

When Dolios woke, the sun was already beating down on the sea.

He sat on the beach with his knees up and told the others what the soldier had said. Every Spartan, he said. Every Thespian. Leonidas.

No one spoke for a while. The water moved against the hull of the boat.

Finally Pallas stood. "We should go."

---

They sailed south. After a time Dolios noticed the sky changing in the way he had come to recognize — the light shifting, deepening, as though the day were older than the hour suggested. Phoebe put her hand in the water and said quietly: we are moving. Not just through the sea.

When they neared the Piraeus, they saw a fleet of ships, but not of the kind they had seen before. This was a motley collection of ships of every size and shape filled with men, women, and children who had clearly never fought in battle. A woman on the nearest vessel — a fishing boat packed with people and furniture and a goat tied to the mast — called across the water when she saw them. Where are you coming from?

Phoebe called back: we have been north. What about you? 

We're returning from Salamis. We've defeated the Persians and they're gone.

"We've only traveled a year forward in time," Kleta said. "It's still 479BCE now. The Persian Wars ran from 490 to 479 — Salamis was last year, in 480, and Plataea this year. They're done."

The woman told them what had happened as the boats moved together toward shore — how Themistocles had sent a secret message to Xerxes saying the Greek fleet was about to scatter, that now was the moment to trap them. How Xerxes believed it and sent his fleet into the narrow strait between Salamis and the mainland, where their numbers became a liability, where the triremes collided in the press, where the Greek ships rowed low and fast and rammed them. How Xerxes watched from a golden throne on the hillside as his fleet burned on the water below him. How he turned for home. How the Persian army that remained was broken the following year at Plataea and the war was done. When the woman finished her story, she turned her gaze toward Athens, and Pallas noticed immediately, that it was filled with sadness and anticipation rather than the joy of victory. 

"Why are you so sad?" Pallas asked. 

"Darling," the woman replied, "Last summer when we had only just arrived and settled into our place of refuge in Salamis, we stood on the shore there and watched Athens burn. The walls are destroyed. The temples are ash. The Persians burned the entire Acropolis before they left. I am not sure life will ever be the same. 

But with that she clutched a small figurine that was tied around her neck. "Who is that?" Phoebe asked. 

"Oh, just a household god," the woman said, "I'm praying that she'll help us rebuild what we've lost." 

---

The woman had not been exagerating. When the children walked into Athens, the Acropolis was an open wound against the sky.

Dolios sat down on a piece of fallen marble at the edge of what had been a marketplace. He was working something out — carefully, the way you work out a sum that will not come out right matter how many times you start over.

"The soldiers at Marathon, at Thermopylae were true heroes. But they weren't able to protect their city from destruction."

An old man nearby — sitting in the shadow of a ruined wall – overheard Dolios. He was cutting a piece of rope, slowly, and carefully for a purpose that was not entirely clear.

"Young man," he said. "The heroes you're talking about were not fighting for a city. Whether a man lays his head on a soft pillow beneath pastoral paintings or on the leaves beneath the open sky, this difference means very little in a man's life. The Greeks turned back the greatest empire in the world so that they could continue, a least for a time, to live as free men. People will be telling this story for a thousand years."

"Quite a bit longer than a thousand years," Kleta said. "Herodotus — whom the Greeks called the Father of History — wrote that free people defeat tyrants because they fight for themselves — for their own lives and laws — while the subjects of a king fight only because they are told to."

The man continued working at his rope and nodded. 

Dolios looked at the ruins. And then looked up to take in the hudreds of men, women, and children who were picking through the rubble, smiling and talking to one another, helping one another gather what they could to start building again.

Pallas had been quiet through all of this but now spoke up. "I think it's time for us to leave, she said, that we've gotten what we came here for. Because I have just realized what the second line of the oracle might mean."  She read the line aloud now, her voice steady.

"Go where the sorrows of kings and peasants churn."

Isn't that here, Phoebe said? In the aftermath of war?

"The sorrows are here," Pallas said, "but not the churn. You see, from every human tragedy the greeks built stories, cycles which they could turn over again and again, like churning butter from cream. I think we need to go find these stories." 

"To find the stories!" Phoebe said. 

The other kids laughed and made their way back to the boat. As they sailed against the small breakers off the Piraeus, Dolios turned around to see the old man working at his rope. The man had perched one foot on a broken pillar and Dolios. What he saw made him rub his eyes to look again, but when his vision cleared the old man was gone. Wings, Dolios, thought. He had wings on his sandle. Hermes, the guide between worlds, had never left me.
