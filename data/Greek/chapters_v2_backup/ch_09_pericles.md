Pericles

The deep eyes of the goddess was the last thing Pallas remembered before she woke again on her couch in the Prytaneion. When she woke she felt she knew Athena better, why she was so industrious but also so fierce. She remembered what the city looked like after Salamis and could see that the Athens built afterward was worth protecting.

When she woke this time, things were not quite the same. The couch was the same; the shape of the room was the same. But the hangings on the walls were a deeper color, newer, and the oil lamps were of a different shape — wider-mouthed, different handles. She noticed the handles before she noticed anything else, and had no idea why.

The priestess was looking at her. And it was the same priestess, she could see by her eyes, but she was much younger than she had been the night before — she was now perhaps twenty-five, with dark hair pulled back and the focused stillness of someone who had been at work for hours. She looked at Pallas with widened eyes, as though she wanted to express surprise but felt it inappropriate to do so.

The others woke in pieces. Dolios first — he came up from sleep the way he came up from underwater, breaking the surface fast and looking around immediately for context. He found it in the hangings and the lamps and then in my not being there.

Socrates is gone, he said.

Klēta asked the priestess directly: what year is it?

It is the archonship of Eucles. Klēta consulted her book and said plainly. It is about 428 BCE, twenty-seven years earlier than when we went to bed.

Does that mean I didn't get eight hours, Dolios said?

The other kids smiled but weren't quite ready for his shenanigans.

---

I should tell you how things went for me, as uneventful as it was. I fell asleep on the couch with the children around me and woke alone. After a few moments the older priestess came and showed me where I could relieve myself, after which she showed me back to the dim room. She offered me a couch near the brightest lamp and a few books she thought I might enjoy. I chose the epic poems of the bard since you can never go wrong with Homer. I set to reading the book in which Hector returns to battle and briefly scares his infant son with his crested helmet. Andromache laughs through her tears, a moment that had always seemed to me the pinnacle of Greek art, comedy showing through our stories of tragedy. I waited, turning this thought over in my mind, grateful for the chance to rest. I suspected I would see the children again in due course.

They came back through the door in the late afternoon, all four of them, with the combined expression of people to whom a great deal had happened.

---

This is what they told me about their journey to an Athens that I remember but am quite happy to forget.

The young priestess was wearing a sad and tired look and when Phoebe inquired gently, she was told. A third of Athens's population had died by plague in the preceding years, with our protector Pericles perishing only a year before — in 429 BCE.

Who was Pericles, Dolios asked me.

I heard him speak many times — in the Assembly, in the Agora, once at the theater between plays. He was not tall, but he carried authority without appearing to carry it, which is the rarest kind.

He came to power when Athens was a democracy by name but not entirely in practice. The old council of the Areopagus, which had governed the city since before memory, still held the real levers. The courts and the Assembly voted, but the vote of a man who needed to be at his workshop by morning counted for less than the vote of a man who owned the workshop. Pericles changed this. He stripped the Areopagus of its political power. He arranged pay for jury service, so that a tanner or a potter could afford to spend a day judging a case alongside a landowner. He opened the public offices to citizens of any class. His argument — which I find half-convincing, which is more than I find most arguments — was that governing a city is not a specialized art requiring the right family. That any Athenian, examined closely, is capable of participating in his own city's decisions.

He also built the Parthenon. He transformed what had begun as the Delian League — a defensive alliance of Greek city-states against Persia — into an Athenian empire. He brought Pheidias here — Pheidias had built the Zeus at Olympia before this, the forty-foot god that people crossed the sea to see — and Sophocles, and Herodotus, and a dozen others I could name. This was what we would later call the Golden Age of Athens. He wanted Athens to be the school of all Greece — his words, from the funeral oration he gave for the first men who died in this war. I heard it. He stood over the graves and said that what we had built here was worth dying for, that future generations would wonder at us. Then the plague took his sons and took him. He faced it without complaint, which tells me he meant what he said.

We need to see for ourselves, Pallas said. And they went.

Pallas told the others about the dream as they walked — the too-white Parthenon, the statue in battle armor, the woman and Arachne. Dolios listened without interrupting, which meant he was paying attention. Klēta asked whether any third party had seen both tapestries before Athena destroyed Arachne's. Pallas said she understood why she was asking but that she probably shouldn't.

The tribute ships were still coming in, they told me. Pericles was dead but the machine he had built kept running. 

All that gold seemed less important with the population dying, Dolios said. As though Athens needed any more monuments...

I told Dolios that we might have done well to be more forgiving of the tribute cities. Athens had become like a king in the old stories who had done Dionysus a small favor and been granted one wish. He wished that everything he touched would turn to gold. The stone turned to gold. The branch turned to gold. He sat down to eat and his food turned to gold. He reached for his daughter.

Stop! Dolios said and rolled his eyes. I would say that Dionysius' gift came with a catch, but this is a catastrophe. 

Yes, I said.

Dolios said he wished the stories would just say the thing instead of making everyone learn it the hard way. 

I'm not sure I would be telling you this story from so many years ago if the author had removed the memorable bits, I told him. 

I suppose not, he said, but I'd really like to meet that Aristophanes guy you mentioned. Someone's got to lighten things up around here. The kids laughed.

This is the part I did not hear until later.

As the children were leaving the Prytaneion that afternoon, Klēta turned around and saw that Phoebe had not followed. She was still inside, standing close to the young priestess, whispering.

Klēta waited by the door. When Phoebe came out, she asked: what were you telling her?

Phoebe walked for a moment before she answered.

I told her she would receive strange travelers, she said, in roughly 27 years, and that she should welcome them. That it would be the last year of her service to the goddess.

Klēta thought about this for a moment.

But she won't remember you told her, she said. By the time she's old enough to be that priestess, she'll have forgotten this whole morning.

I don't think so, Phoebe said. This is the kind of message a priestess doesn't forget.

She was right. The woman who had let us in the night before — who had whispered with Phoebe while the rest of us slept, who had told us that the goddess had long ago promised her she would receive important guests — had known we were coming for twenty-seven years. For all she knew, the girl who delivered her the message and returned 27 years later without aging a day was indeed a goddess.
