The Golden Age

The last lamp was still burning when Pallas woke.

The others were asleep — Dolios with one arm hanging to the floor, Klēta curled around her book. The priestess moved quietly among them, checking a wick. When she saw that Pallas was awake, she did not say anything. She tilted her head toward the door.

Pallas followed her through the sequence of corridors they had entered the night before — the narrow passage, the anteroom, the outer hall — and then they were in the street. Athens, early dawn, the sky still gray but beginning to lighten in the east.

The priestess went back inside. Pallas stood alone.

She felt the pull before she understood it — something like the feeling before a question, when you know there is a question coming but can't quite say what it is. She began walking. Not toward the harbor. Up.

She would tell me later that the streets felt different as she climbed — the buildings too clean, the plaster too fresh, the light between them carrying a warmth that didn't match the hour. She thought at first she was simply tired, and then she thought she was dreaming, and then she stopped thinking about it and kept walking, because the pull upward was stronger than the question.

---

She came around a corner and it was there.

The Parthenon. White. Not honey-colored, not weathered — white, the way marble looks when it has just been cut, before the sun has had years to work on it. In my time, in the time the children had left, the Parthenon stands as it has always stood, old enough that the eye passes over it. What Pallas saw had never been looked at long enough for that. The columns threw shadows that seemed too precise. The gilded spear tip of the statue inside caught the first light from the east and returned it. Pallas stood and looked at it for a long time.

When she started again, she went up.

The path wound along the south face of the rock — steep, the stones worn smooth in the center from years of feet. The city spread below her as she rose: rooftops and cooking fires, the Agora beginning to stir, the harbor and the silver-gray surface of the sea.

At the top she walked through the great entrance and stopped.

The statue was ahead of her. Bronze, not marble — larger than she had expected, the scale of it wrong in the way that some things are wrong when you meet them: taller than the buildings she had grown up with, the helmet adding another story. Athena in full armor. Shield on her left arm, spear raised in her right. The goddess of wisdom, the goddess of craft and weaving, standing dressed for war.

A woman was arranging an offering at the base — a small tripod, some grain in a clay dish. Perhaps forty, with the unhurried manner of someone who had come here many times.

Why is she dressed for battle? Pallas asked.

The woman looked up. She took in Pallas — a thirteen-year-old girl, well-dressed, unaccompanied, staring at the statue with the expression of someone who has just noticed a contradiction.

She's made of Marathon, the woman said. The bronze came from the shields and helmets the Persians left on the plain. Athena makes what the city needs. She gave us the olive tree and the shuttle and the art of building ships. When the Persians came, she made what was needed to defend all of that. After Marathon, after Salamis, we built all of this. She gestured toward the Parthenon, its columns catching the early light. But she kept the armor. Athena Promachos — she who fights in the front ranks. Because what you build is only worth building if you can defend it.

Pallas looked up at the raised spear.

Is Athena kind to artists? she asked.

The woman set down her tripod and thought for a moment.

Did anyone ever tell you about Arachne? she said.

---

Arachne was a weaver, the woman told her. From Lydia — you'd know the textiles, the deep reds, the close patterns. Arachne was the best of them. So good that people stopped on the road to watch her work through the window of the workshop. She said publicly — without much ceremony, as a statement of fact — that she was better than Athena.

Athena came to her in the form of an old woman and gave her the chance to take it back. Arachne didn't take it back.

They held a contest. Athena wove a tapestry showing the gods in their glory: the twelve Olympians enthroned, mortals who had challenged them broken in the corners, the right order of things made visible in thread.

Arachne wove the gods as they appear in the stories. Zeus, a bull, pursuing Europa. Zeus, a swan. Zeus, a shower of gold falling through a window. Poseidon deceiving Medusa. Apollo going after girls who ran from him. Every myth where a god had taken what they wanted from someone who could not refuse. She wove them perfectly, one after the next, the figures exact, the work even, not a flaw in it.

The woman paused. She picked up her clay dish and straightened.

What happened to the tapestry? Pallas asked.

Athena destroyed it. Struck it with her shuttle until it tore. Then she struck Arachne and transformed her into a spider, so she could keep weaving but never again insult the gods.

Was her work inferior? Pallas asked.

That's the wrong question, my dear. The gods powerful and eternal. They are kind and cruel to whomever they choose. Even the greatests artists must remember who they are.

She picked up her things and left. Pallas stood alone.

She walked around to the far end of the Parthenon. The west pediment showed Athena and Poseidon at the moment of contest: the salt spring on one side, the olive tree on the other, both gods poised above their gifts, the city's elders arranged between them like witnesses. Athens had chosen the olive tree. Poseidon had flooded the plain in response. The marble showed only the moment before the decision — suspended in stone, neither outcome yet arrived.

She went back to the statue and looked at its face.

The craftsman had hollowed the eyes and set something in the sockets — stone or glass or ivory — that gave them depth. The expression was fixed at something between watchfulness and warning, the way a face looks when the thought behind it has had time to settle. Pallas looked into the eyes for a long time trying to discover who it was that was looking back.
