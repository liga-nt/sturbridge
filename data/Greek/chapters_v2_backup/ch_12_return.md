We Owe a Cock to Asclepius

I should tell you something about how this story came to exist.

When the children left me that evening — Phoebe naming the oracle's last line, my trial beginning the next morning — I sat for a long time with the lamp and did not sleep. In the weeks since the charges were filed, I had been following a dream I kept having: a figure who appeared and told me to make music. I had taken this as a command to do philosophy in verse, and I had spent my mornings composing hymns to Apollo and putting Aesop's fables into meter. It was not very good poetry. I am not a poet. I am, as I have often said, not particularly accomplished at most things.

But after the children left, I understood what the dream had actually meant.

The story that needed to be told was not a hymn. It was the one I had been living since four children appeared in the Agora carrying a flat book and a boat that sailed against the wind. I had been a witness to something, and a witness has an obligation. I took a fresh scroll and, despite my life long refusal to put thoughts down on paper, I wrote.

This story is what you have been reading and there is a little more I can put down before it must, like all things, come to an end.

---

The trial was on the fifth day of the month Thargelion, before a jury of five hundred and one citizens. The children came and stood as close to the enclosure as they could get. Pallas told me later that she could see my face throughout. I am not sure whether to find this reassuring.

The charges were impiety and corrupting the youth. Meletus spoke for the prosecution with the particular energy of a man who wants to win rather than to be understood. Then I stood to speak.

I will not reproduce the whole argument here — Plato will no doubt give a better account than I could and is determined despite my protestations to do so, in the work he intends to call my Apology. What I told the jury was approximately this: I am not wise. I have never claimed to be. The oracle said no one in Athens was wiser than I, and the only explanation I could find, after years of investigation, was that I at least knew what I did not know, while every general and poet and craftsman I interviewed believed they knew considerably more than they did. I had been pointing this out for forty years, which had made me unpopular in the way that pointing out unwelcome things tends to do. I was not going to stop. If the court would acquit me on the condition that I cease, I would respectfully decline. The god required this work of me, and I was more afraid of disobeying the god than any jury.

I am told this is not the customary approach to defending oneself against capital crimes.

They found me guilty, two hundred and eighty to two hundred and twenty. The difference was a lot smaller than I thought it would be.

Then came the penalty. Meletus proposed death. I was expected to propose an alternative, and I gave it genuine thought. What had I earned for forty years of service to the examined life, performed at considerable personal expense? By my own reckoning: to be fed at public expense in the Prytaneion, in the company of the city's Olympic victors and other benefactors.

There was a commotion when I announced this suggestion, not least among my closest friends. I saw Plato's face go entirely white. I think it was at that moment that he knew what was to come. 

He and the others begged me to propose a fine instead. I offered one mina, which was what I had. Plato and Crito and several others offered thirty, on their personal guarantee, and I passed this promise onto the jury. But by that time, the jury had made their decision. They chose death, by a larger margin than the guilty verdict. Even those who thought me innocent must have been offended by manner during the sentencing.

I told them as they filed out that I bore them no ill but suspected that I would be more troublesome to them in death than I had been even in life. If they had waited but a few years, I would have made my exit from this shadowy place we call life with little protest or fanfare.

---

The sacred ship had sailed for Delos the day before the trial. By Athenian law, no execution could proceed until it returned — the god's business came before the city's. This took thirty days. The children stayed.

Each morning they came with the others — Crito, Simmias, Cebes, Phaedo, and various students who had been with me for years. A guard who had developed a fondness for Klēta waved them through each day without comment. They sat against the wall and listened while we conversed, which is all we had ever done in any circumstance, and which seemed as reasonable a way as any to spend a morning.

Crito came alone one morning before the others. The children were sleeping nearby and something in his manner made them follow at a distance.

He had a plan. Friends in Thessaly. Guards who were not unsympathetic and could be persuaded with money, of which there was enough. The ship had not yet returned. It could be arranged.

I asked him to sit down.

What he wanted, I told him, was to save my life. What he would actually accomplish, if I fled, was to demonstrate to everyone in Athens that I had decided, at the very end, that my own comfort mattered more than the laws I had spent my life asking others to respect. Every argument I had ever made about the citizen's obligation to the city — gone. Available to anyone who wanted to use it against my students, against him, against everything we had built together in conversation over forty years. I declined.

He had no counter-argument. He sat quietly for some time and then went home.

The children asked me afterward whether I had been tempted.

To be tempted, I said, would be to suppose that life is somehow better than death. I had always been of the opinion that life was a preparation for death and that it made little sense to prepare poorly.
---

The ship was sighted off Piraeus on the morning of the thirtieth day. The children came before dawn and spoke to the guard, who shook his head — only the inner circle, the friends of long standing.

Pallas told him they had been there every morning for a month.

He looked at all four of them for a moment. Then he stood aside.

The room was quiet. Several friends were already along the walls. I was awake, as I had been for some hours. As I write these words, the children are approaching me. I lifted my hand to show that I need but a moment more. And that is not to offer you any more words, but to ask the god's blessing on my work. I pray that the god not be offended by my efforts, humble as they are. I pray further that these words might help the children gain steadier passage through the trials life, or at the very least, do them no harm.

---

*Pallas*

When he finished that last sentence, Socrates rolled the scroll and handed it to Klēta. It was Klēta who decided that we should each write our own notes at the bottom to finish the story. She called them annotations and decided what order we should write.

So I'll tell you a bit more about that morning. Socrates explained how he'd been commanded in a dream to write philosophy in verse. When he found that the poetry wasn't very good, he wondered whether he had interpreted his dream correctly. He said, I decided then to write about the only genuinely extraordinary thing that has happened to me in recent memory. You children came, you asked good questions, and you received an answer and let that answer guide you, outward to parts of the world I have never seen, but also inward. I have an inner voice, a daimon, that always tells me if I am thinking of doing something wrong. Through the hours that I was writing your story, my daimon was completely silent. Now, I give it to you as a farewell gift. 

But our answer didn't provide any answers, Klēta said. We still don't know what our city, our world, must do to find harmony and peace.

No, he said. Neither do I.

I looked around and several of the people in the room were weeping. Among them was Dolios, tears streaming down his face. And then he did something that surpised us all, but didn't seem to surprise Socrates. He lifted his fist up at Socrates, his face red, and said: "You are doing this! You are doing this! You don't have to do this." And he collapsed in the arms of Crito who had been quietly weeping beside him.

Socrates turned to me and said, I think it is time for you children to go. You have a long journey ahead of you. 

We can stay, I told him. I'm not afraid. 

Of course not, Socrates said, but it's time. See here even Plato has dispensed with the drama. He knows the story has come to an end and that we shouldn't belabor the ending of the story the way the less skillful poets tend to do.

I think he might feel the way that Dolios feels, Phoebe said. Socrates looked down slightly and said, I think you might be right.

As we were reluctanly moving toward the door, Dolios' heaving chest had stilled, and he looked back at Socrates sitting quitely on his couch. He pulled away from us and ran to him. He hugged Socrates so tightly, it looked like he might never let go, and Socrates laid his hand gently on Dolios' back. He leaned toward his ear and whispered something. A few seconds later, Dolios liften his head from Socrates' chest without looking at him and ran back to us. Ever since, I've wanted to ask him what Socrates said, but haven't felt it was my place to do so.

---

*Phoebe*

While we were leaving, we heard Socrates speak loudly to Crito. Crito, he said, We owe a cock to Asclepius. I knew well that Asclepius was the god of healing and it seemed strange that Socrates would sacrifice to the god of healing just before he died of poison. But it showed just how differently Socrates saw death than any of us did. He saw it as a type of healing for all the difficulties and darknesses of life.

It also made me think that we needed to honor the gods and goddesses in our own way. We had arrived in Athens through Poseidon's storm and sailed his water to every place the oracle sent us. Athens had belonged to Athena since she offered the olive tree, which was the better gift. But the sea had been ours to use, and you should not set sail on any sea, no matter how good your rigging, without doing homage to Poseidon first. 

I bought a small oil cake from a woman near the docks and put it in the water at the harbor's edge. I said what seemed right: that we had traveled far, that we were grateful, and that we were going home.

Dolios said: do you think that'll actually work?

I said I didn't know. But it seemed right to ask.

---

*Dolios*

It did not work.

The storm came three hours out of Piraeus, from the east, without building — just there, all at once, the way our first storm had come. Pallas took the tiller and Klēta the ropes and I held on to what was solid and tried to be useful. It was not a large storm in the way the open sea is large. It was violent in a more personal way.

I am not going to say I was afraid, because I was not exactly afraid. I had been at Thermopylae and watched three hundred men stay in a pass they knew would kill them. I had been in plague Athens. I had met a soldier in Alexandria who marched to the edge of the world and came home and mended nets on a pier. I had said goodbye that morning to the wisest man I ever met and watched him facing death without the slightest worry.

You get a sense, after a while, what the appropriate size of a feeling is.

The storm passed. The stars came out wrong, and then right. The water went from dark blue to grey-green.

---

*Klēta*

Phoebe saw the cove first. She always did.

We came in on the tide and tied off where we had found the boat, at the same cleat, in the same quiet water. We had been gone, in our own time, no more than an hour, or perhaps, no time at all.

I was carrying the scrolls from Alexandria and the scroll Socrates had given us. On the walk up from the cove I thought about the oracle's last line — the one Phoebe had named, the widest sea meeting the land. I had thought it meant Socrates: his widest mind the sea, his death the final shore. Now I thought it also meant this: that our own questioning hearts were the widest sea, and the land was not out there in the world, it was home. 

I knew from Thucydides that nothing is ever quite over. An author writes her last sentence and someone else picks up the pen. The Library understood this — shelves that kept going past what any one person could read in a lifetime. The oracle had four lines and we followed them all, which should have felt like a conclusion. It didn't feel like the end, but it felt like a place to return to, and to rest.

We walked up from the cove and did not look back.
