What the Maker Made

They came back from the harbor in the late morning, quieter than I expected.

Dolios had the look of a boy who had seen something he couldn't yet find the words for. Pallas was walking fast. Klēta was talking quietly to Phoebe. I fell in beside them without asking any questions, which cost me something — I don't mind the silence, but when questions rise up in me, I'm not used to stifling them.

We were heading to the theater. Sophocles — who in forty years of competition had never been judged lower than second place — had written a play as his last, called Oedipus at Colonus, about the exiled king of Thebes, and had not lived to see it performed. His grandson was putting it on this season. I had been meaning to go.

We walked for a while without speaking. Then, because children looked like they needed some kind of thread to follow, I spoke up. You might wondering about the oracle's answer, I said. The oracle doesn't tell you where to go or what to do. Her answers are more like a labyrinth – a maze that helps you escape your problem by forcing you to find a path out. 

How does being trapped help you find a way out, Dolios said?

I think it would be best if I told you about the most famous labyrinth among our stories. Would you like hear it.

All of the kids nodded somewhat vacantly, their eyes tired but still searching.

---

There was a craftsman named Daedalus, I began. He was the finest builder in the world — not the richest, not the most famous in his own time, but the finest, in the sense that what he built actually worked. He was Athenian by birth, though he spent most of his life elsewhere, which is perhaps what Athens does to its best people.

His trouble began, as trouble often does, with a king making a small dishonest decision that got very large very quickly. Minos, the king of Crete, had received a magnificent white bull from Poseidon as a divine gift — the understanding being that he would sacrifice it in return. He didn't. He liked the bull too much. Poseidon, who was not deceived and had a long memory, cursed Minos's wife Pasiphae to fall in love with it.

Now Daedalus enters the story. Pasiphae came to him with a commission that most craftsmen would have declined, and he built it: a hollow wooden cow, covered in hide, convincing enough to fool the bull. The result of this forbidden love was the Minotaur — half man, half bull — which Minos could neither kill nor set loose, so he went back to Daedalus and asked him to build something to put it in.

Daedalus built the labyrinth.

Was it beautiful? Pallas asked.

This stopped me. I said I had honestly never thought about it that way. I supposed that from above — if you could see the whole pattern at once — it might be extraordinary. Every passage feeding into the next, every turn calculated, a kind of argument made out of stone. But of course no one could see it that way. From inside there was only the wall in front of you.

She nodded slowly, thinking about something.

Now, Minos was already in a blood feud with Athens. His son Androgeos had recently come to Athens and defeated us in every Olympic contest. Our king was envious and frightened of Androgeos' strength and sent him to fight a terrible monster at Marathon, knowing that he would die.

Marathon! Dolios said.

The very one, I replied. But Marathon on this day was the site of Athens' shame rather than its pride. The heroic Androgeos was slaughtered by the monster, and when Minos heard the fate of his son, he demanded a high price: seven young men and seven young women, delivered as tribute from Athens every year. These Athenians, in the bloom of youth, were to be fed into the Minotaur's labyrinth. For several years, the joyful sounds of spring in Athens were replace with sounds of agony and grief – parents being torn from their children. Finally the king's son Theseus could no longer stand by and watch. Despite his privilege as a royal, he demanded to be included in the tribute, though in disguise. He sailed to Crete. Minos's daughter Ariadne fell in love with him — this happens a great deal in these stories — and she went to Daedalus and asked how Theseus might survive the labyrinth. Daedalus told her about a ball of thread.

Theseus unraveled it as he went in. He found the Minotaur. He killed it. He followed the thread back out.

Then he sailed home and forgot to change the sail.

Dolios said: he forgot to change the sail?

His father, I said. His father Aegeus had made Theseus promise: raise a white sail coming home if you're alive, a black sail if you're not. Theseus forgot. His father saw the black sail coming over the horizon, concluded his son was dead, and threw himself into the sea. 

The Aegean Sea, Klēta said quietly. She had opened the map of Greece on her lap.

Yes, I said. 

Pallas was still thinking about something, I could tell. She had a way of going quiet that meant she was turning a thing over, looking for where to take hold of it.

She said: what happened to Daedalus?

Minos was furious that Daedalus had helped Theseus escape. He imprisoned him — and his son Icarus — inside the labyrinth. Which meant that Daedalus now lived inside the thing he had built, and could not get out of it. I have thought about this part of the story more than the rest of it. He knew every passage. He had designed every dead end. And he still couldn't find the door.

Dolios said: is there any story in Athens that ends well? Any of them at all? I would very much like to hear one. Or at least get some comic relief. I mean come on old man.

Dolios infectious energy filled us all with laughter and I marveled that he could provide us the very thing he demanded – a mark of deep sincerity in my book. Well, there is always Aristophanes, I began, but Pallas interrupted.

You haven't told us what happened to Daedalus, she said.

Well, yes, he couldn't unravel his own puzzle but he trusted in his art. Since he couldn't get out, he went up. 

He built wings, Pallas said.

Yes. He watched birds for a long time — the way he watched everything — and then he built two pairs of wings from feathers and wax. One for himself, one for Icarus. He was careful about the instructions. Not too low, he said: the spray will weigh the feathers down. Not too high: the sun will melt the wax. He had Icarus repeat it back to him.

They flew out of Crete together. Daedalus kept to the middle height. He looked back once. Then he turned and watched the horizon ahead, which was perhaps the one thing he should not have done.

Icarus flew higher. And then higher still. His father could see the joy he felt at flying so freely but knew that he was also flying out of reach and that with all his ingenuity he had nothing with which to catch him.

Come on, Dolios said. I mean come on.

The wax melted. I said. And he fell.

The Icarian Sea, Kleta said. She showed the other kids on the map.

Did Daedalus continue building after Icarus died? Kleta asked.

The story is that he continued to carve and build and sculpt as it took his mind off his sorrow, but one day when he was pounding out a frieze telling the story of Minos and Labyrinth, of the Athenian tribute, of Theseus and Ariadne, he came to the part where he was meant to fashion a relief of his son flying ahead of him toward the sun. But each time he raised his hand to fashion it, his strength failed him. And finally he left the frieze unfinished.

The children were quiet as I finished my story. Gradually, the theater came into view ahead — stone tiers curved against the south face of the Acropolis, catching the midmorning sun. A crowd was already moving up along the approach, slow and unhurried, the way Athenians move toward something they have been waiting for.
