The Storm

Before I recount the children's journey to Delphi, I need to explain what I later came to know about the manner in which they arrived in Athens. I did not see what I am about to describe. I want to say that plainly. For a man writing about a storm he did not stand inside is already at a disadvantage, and the children told me this story in pieces over many days, sometimes contradicting one another about which wave came first or who was at the tiller when the sky changed. So what follows is my reconstruction. Take it as such.

They had taken the boat without permission. This much is certain, because all four of them admitted it independently, and children who agree on the wrongness of a thing they did together are usually telling the truth. Their world — the one they had not yet described to me in detail — was, by their accounts, loud and frightening and full of people shouting past each other. Pallas said she simply wanted an hour of quiet. Dolios said he wanted to see how fast the boat would go. Klēta said she wanted to test some adjustment she had made to the rigging. Phoebe said she went because the others were going and she did not trust them to come back safely without her.

The sea, that morning, was not the Aegean. It was a cove near a village called Orleans, on a long arm of land that curls into a vast ocean they aptly call the Atlantic. They had sailed these coves a hundred times, Phoebe told me — a place of pale green water and sandy bottom and low dunes covered in a twisted scrub that is nothing like our olive groves – different in the way that small beloved landscapes are like themselves and nothing else. The air smelled of salt and something else she could not name, something colder. They launched the boat in the morning light. The water was shallow enough that they could see the bottom. It was, she said, the most familiar water in the world.

For perhaps an hour they were happy.

Klēta sat in the bow with her feet up and read. She had brought a book in her canvas bag — a history of the ancient Greeks, thick and illustrated, which she had been working through all summer. Dolios kept drifting toward her, trying to read over her shoulder, and asking her questions about famous battles: who commanded whom, how you rose from one rank to the next, what a general actually had to do to become a general. He had dropped a book in the water once so Klēta would not let him hold it.

While the kids were carrying on, the sky went black.

I asked them, more than once, to describe how it happened, because I have lived by the sea all my life and I know how storms gather. They build. You see them coming for an hour, sometimes a day. This storm, they said, did not build. It arrived. One moment the horizon was clean, and the next a wall of cloud stood over them, black as wet stone, and the wind that had been carrying them began to push them sideways and then up and then down in a way that wind is not supposed to do.

Pallas took the tiller. Klēta went to the ropes. Dolios, who in calmer moments would have made a joke, looked worried. Phoebe alone went still. She told me later she had an instinct she could not explain — that the right response to a voice was to listen to it. So she listened, while the sea tried to take them. She remembered thinking the wind had a voice — not a sound, she said, but a voice, the way a person has a voice — and that the voice was not angry with them exactly, but was paying attention to them in a way she did not want to be paid attention to.

This is the point at which I must stop and tell you what I think happened, though I will not insist on it. You see, we Athenians have a name for that voice. It is the voice of Poseidon, who shakes the earth and lifts the sea, and does not always announce himself with anything but a sudden change in the weather. We represent him astride a massive horse and carrying a trident – a staff with three ends. The children had not known that there is a god whose business is the sea, and when they learned that from me, they were quiet for some time.

As they struggled at the ropes, the waves became walls. The boat was thrown. Phoebe said at one point she looked up and saw water above her — not a wave breaking, but water suspended over the boat as though the sea had decided, briefly, to become the sky. Dolios was washed off the deck and caught by a rope around his ankle and hauled himself back aboard with both hands and, he claims, his teeth. Pallas stayed at the tiller. Klēta, who is small and precocious, had the wherewithal to tie herself to the mast.

And then, as suddenly as it had risen, it ceased.

The wind fell off all at once, and the sea, which had been mountainous, lay down flat as a beaten bronze plate. The sun burned away the dark cloud, and a coastline emerged the shape of which they had ever seen before.

Pallas knew it first. She said the water had changed color — from the pale green she had grown up sailing, shallow and sandy-bottomed and mild, to a deep blue that meant depth. Rocky hills rose from the shore, covered in low silver-leafed trees. The smell of the air was different. Phoebe said she understood, standing there at the prow, exactly what Odysseus must have felt when he came to himself on the beach of the Phaeacians: that he had been carried a very great distance by something that did not ask his permission, and that the road home would not be the road he had come in on.

She did not yet know the name Odysseus. But she understood the shape of the thing.

Like Odysseus, they sailed toward the land because there was nowhere else to sail, and because the boat — and here is what I want to pause on — the boat was undamaged. Sail intact. Rigging intact. That extraordinary rope-pattern which I mentioned earlier was undisturbed. Whatever had happened to them, the vessel had been spared.

They washed up on a beach east of Piraeus. When they climbed out of the boat, they did not yet know what year it was, or what country, or what god had had them in his hand. They only knew that they were alive, and that the world they had left was somehow no longer the world they were standing in. Quiet drammatically Dolios kissed the ground and then stood up smiling at the idea that he might meet the inhabitants of a strange and novel land.

They walked up toward the city and that's where they found me and Plato and Glaucon. I would not hear this part of the story until much later — told to me in pieces, at the dock at Piraeus, on the morning I watched them sail away. By the time I understood how they had arrived, they were already gone again, headed north toward the oracle. And I know now, though I would not have said so then, that I was already waiting for the day a small boat would round the western tip of Attica with these four small adventurers who were, to be sure, on a journy of their own but also welcome companions on the last stretch of mine.
